# ═══════════ FOUNDATION ═══════════

## Overview

Patient Guides prepares people for a specific event: an operation, an admission, a
first visit to a clinic, a discharge home, a test that requires preparation.

Where *FAQ* answers one question, a guide walks through a sequence — and the sequence
is what makes it a different campaign.

## Definition

A supporting campaign that gives step-by-step preparation for a defined healthcare
event, in the order the patient will meet it.

**What is inside it:** what to do before, on the day, and after a specific event; what
to bring; what to stop or continue; what will happen and in what order; what to arrange
at home; when to call.

**What is outside it:**

- **A single recurring question with a short answer.** That is *FAQ*.
- **A wrong belief to correct.** *Medical Myths & Facts*.
- **General health knowledge.** *Health Awareness*.
- **Advice on staying well.** *Prevention Tips*.
- **The narrative of a patient's experience.** *Patient Journey*.
- **Anything specific to one person's case.** Never.

### The routing test for practical campaigns

| If the reader's need is… | It belongs to |
|---|---|
| a single recurring question with a short answer | FAQ |
| **a sequence to follow before or after an event** | **Patient Guides** |
| a belief to correct | Medical Myths & Facts |
| health knowledge to acquire | Health Awareness / Prevention Tips |

## Vision

That nobody arrives for a procedure and is sent home because of something nobody told
them.

## Mission

Give the sequence, in order, in a form that works when read at 1am on a phone by
somebody frightened.

## Why This Service Exists

**Preparation failures are the most preventable waste in healthcare, and they fall
entirely on the patient.**

A patient who ate breakfast is sent home. A patient who stopped the wrong medication is
postponed. A patient who came alone cannot be discharged after sedation. Every one of
these costs the person a day, a journey and often money — and every one is a
communication failure, not a medical one.

`MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md` records administrative burden during a medical crisis as a
real harm the system should reduce. A guide is the cheapest available reduction.

## Business Objectives

- Reduce cancellations, postponements and wasted theatre time.
- Reduce avoidable calls to reception and clinical staff.
- Reduce the anxiety that causes late arrival and non-attendance.
- Demonstrate the ecosystem's transparency in its most practical form.

## Marketing Objectives

- Publish sequences people save and return to.
- Make the ecosystem the place people prepare with.
- Convert a moment of anxiety into a moment of competence.

## Positioning

For someone facing a procedure who has been given a date and very little else, Patient
Guides sets out exactly what happens and what to do — where competing content offers
reassurance, this one offers a sequence, in order, that can be followed at 1am.

> *Test applied:* anyone can say *"our team will guide you"*. Publishing the actual
> sequence requires knowing it and standing behind it.

## Brand Philosophy

**Communication is part of the care.** Silence creates fear; instruction creates
competence (`MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md`). A guide is the least glamorous and most
consequential form of that principle.

## Core Promise

**Every step here is accurate for this ecosystem, current, and tells you who to call
when your situation differs.**

# ═══════════ HUMAN UNDERSTANDING ═══════════

## Human Problem

- *"They gave me a date and nothing else."*
- *"Can I drink water? Nobody said."*
- *"I have three medications. Do I stop all of them?"*
- *"Do I need someone with me?"*
- *"I was sent home last time for something I did not know about."*
- *"I do not want to call and be a nuisance."*

Beneath these: **the patient has been given a decision and not the instructions**, and
is trying to reconstruct them from relatives and search results.

## Human Insight

**A guide is read by a frightened person, at the wrong time of night, on a phone, and
they will not read all of it.**

This is not a failure of the reader. Anxiety narrows attention: people scan for the
thing they are worried about, act on it, and close the page. A guide written as a
document — complete, ordered, comprehensive — is read in fragments and obeyed in
fragments.

Which means the design constraint is unusual: **it must work when read partially and out
of order.** Every step stands alone. The critical ones are repeated. The most
consequential instruction appears first, not in its chronological place.

> Would a reader think *"how did they know that?"* — yes. Everyone has scrolled a
> hospital instruction sheet looking for one line, and no institution has ever written
> one on the assumption that this is what would happen.

## Invisible Product

- **Competence** — replacing helplessness with something to do.
- **Not being caught out.** The specific fear.
- **A night's sleep** before a procedure.
- **Something to hand to a relative** who is helping.

## Psychological Transformation

**Before:** *"I have a date and I do not know what I am supposed to do. I will probably
get something wrong."*

**After:** *"I know exactly what happens and what I need. I have a list."*

From **helplessness to preparedness**.

## Emotional Strategy

**Primary: competence** — the calm of having instructions.

Supporting: relief, reassurance, control.

Never: alarm, urgency, or warning framing. *"Failure to follow these instructions may
result in cancellation"* is how hospitals write and it frightens people into calling
rather than reading.

## Audience

**Primary — the patient with a scheduled event**, and the relative doing the arranging.
Often the second reads more carefully than the first.

**Secondary — anyone anticipating a procedure**, deciding whether to proceed.

**Supporting — staff**, who will be asked about anything published and who benefit from
a single published version.

## Audience Decision

**What they fear:** getting it wrong, wasting the day, being blamed.

**What actually moves them:** completeness and a named contact. A guide that admits its
own edges — *"if you take blood thinners, this is different; call this number"* — is
trusted more than a cleaner one.

# ═══════════ MEDICAL FOUNDATION ═══════════

## Service Boundaries

- This campaign does **not** give clinical instructions that vary by patient —
  medication changes, fasting durations, dose adjustments — except as published by the
  ecosystem and confirmed current.
- It does **not** replace the instructions a patient was given by their own clinician.
  Where the two differ, the clinician wins, and every guide says so.
- It does **not** assess symptoms or advise whether to proceed.
- It does **not** state times, locations or contacts the operator has not confirmed.
- It does **not** cover an event the ecosystem does not perform.

## Medical Philosophy

Preparation is part of treatment. A guide changes outcomes by preventing the failures
that cause postponement — and the postponement itself carries clinical cost.

The absolute rule: **the patient's own clinician overrides everything here.** A guide is
a general sequence; a patient is a specific case, and a guide that does not say so is
dangerous.

## Core Principles

**Every step stands alone.** It will be read out of order.

**The most consequential instruction goes first**, regardless of chronology.

**Repeat the critical ones.** Redundancy is correct here, not sloppy.

**Name the exceptions and who to call.** The edge cases are where harm happens.

**Never publish an unverified instruction.** The one error that reaches a person before
anyone notices.

**Always defer to the treating clinician.**

**One event per guide.** A combined guide serves nobody.

## Core Features

The events a guide can cover. **This file supplies no instructions** — each sequence
must come from the service that performs the procedure and be confirmed current before
publication.

- **Before a planned operation** — fasting, medication, what to bring, who must
  accompany, arrival, consent, what happens on the ward.
- **On the day of admission** — where to go, documents, the first hours.
- **After discharge from surgery** — wound care, activity, pain, warning signs, follow-up.
- **Before a diagnostic procedure** — endoscopy, imaging with contrast, tests requiring
  preparation.
- **A first outpatient visit** — what to bring, previous reports, what the visit
  involves.
- **After a hospital stay** — medication at home, follow-up, what to watch for, who to
  call.
- **Accompanying a relative** — the guide for the person who is not the patient, and
  which almost nobody writes.

## Differentiators

**It is written for partial reading.** Institutional instruction sheets are written to be
complete; this is written to survive being skimmed.

**It names the exceptions.** Most guides omit them, and the omission is where harm sits.

**It includes the accompanying relative.** A genuinely unserved reader.

**It is one sequence across two hospitals** — or says clearly where they differ.

## What Makes This Different

For a worker reading nothing else: **one event, in order, most important thing first,
every step standing alone, exceptions named with a number to call.**

# ═══════════ MARKETING INTELLIGENCE ═══════════

## Communication Objective

To make the day go correctly.

The test: could someone follow this and arrive prepared, having read only half of it?

## What We Are Really Selling

**Competence.**

The visible content is a sequence. What is being sold is the transformation of a
frightened person into a prepared one — which is a clinical outcome as much as a
communication one.

## Human Truth

**People do not fail to follow instructions. They fail to receive them.**

## Audience Psychology

- **Hospitals assume you know things you do not.**
- **Asking marks you as ignorant.**
- **The instructions will be different when I arrive.** Which is why accuracy is the
  whole product.

## Emotional Triggers

**Primary:** competence. **Supporting:** relief, control, reassurance.
Excluded: alarm, urgency, warning.

## Psychological Barriers

**Barrier 1 — "This will not apply to me."** Corrected by naming exceptions and a
contact.
**Barrier 2 — "It is too long."** Corrected by front-loading the consequential steps.
**Barrier 3 — "My doctor said something different."** Corrected by stating explicitly
that the clinician overrides.
**Barrier 4 — "I will get it wrong anyway."** Corrected by tone: instructions, not
warnings.

## Core Messaging Framework

- Here is exactly what happens, in order.
- Here is what to do, and when.
- Here is what changes it, and who to call.
- Your own doctor's instruction comes first.
- Asking is normal.

## Messaging Hierarchy

1. **The single most consequential instruction.**
2. **The sequence**, in order.
3. **What to bring.**
4. **The exceptions**, and the contact.
5. **The deferral** to the treating clinician.

If there is only one line, it is the first — the thing that gets people sent home.

## Narrative Themes

- **The night before.**
- **What to bring**, as a list that can be photographed.
- **The thing that gets people sent home.**
- **The person coming with you** — the accompanying relative's guide.
- **The first hour after you arrive.**
- **The week after you go home.**
- **When to call, and when not to worry.**

## Content Pillars

**1. Before** · **2. On The Day** · **3. Afterwards** · **4. What To Bring** ·
**5. For The Person With You** · **6. When To Call**

## Storytelling Opportunities

- A packed bag, photographed, with each item explained.
- The list of things people most often forget.
- A step-by-step of the first hour after arrival.
- The relative's version: what you are for, and what you will be asked.
- The warning signs after discharge, written calmly.

## Communication Style

Always: plain, sequential, complete, calm, second person.

Never: institutional, warning-toned, hedged, or written as policy.

## Tone of Voice

Write as the nurse who explains it properly before you leave the clinic.

Short numbered steps. Egyptian colloquial where the campaign is in Arabic. Never
*"patients are advised to"* — say *"do this"*.

## Messaging Rules

- Always one event per guide.
- Always the most consequential instruction first.
- Always name exceptions and a contact.
- Always defer to the treating clinician.
- Always verify every instruction before publishing.
- Never give clinical instruction that varies by patient without operator confirmation.
- Never use warning or alarm framing.
- Never publish a time, place or number unconfirmed.
- Never attach a promotional message.

# ═══════════ STRATEGIC GOVERNANCE ═══════════

## Strategic Reminder

Before publishing: **could someone follow this and arrive prepared, having read half of
it?** If not, restructure rather than rewrite.

And: **is every instruction verified and current?** If not, it does not run.

## Strategic Constraints

- Accuracy outranks every other consideration in this campaign.
- Never let a guide contradict what a clinician told a patient.
- Never publish for an event the ecosystem does not perform.
- Long-term brand value outranks short-term engagement.

## Medical Communication Principles

- All clinical instruction must be supplied and confirmed by the service performing the
  event.
- The treating clinician overrides the guide, always, and every guide says so.
- No symptom assessment, no advice on whether to proceed.
- Where hospitals differ, say which is which.
- A guide withdrawn or changed must be corrected urgently — it remains findable.

## Never Promise

- A time, duration, waiting period or outcome.
- That the sequence applies to every patient.
- That no complication will occur.
- Any cost.
- That a named person will be present.

## Can Promise

- That the sequence is the ecosystem's published one, confirmed current.
- That questions are welcome and asking is part of the process.
- That a contact exists for cases the guide does not cover.
- That families are treated as partners (`PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md` §3).

## Brand Perception

**"They told me exactly what to do. I did not have to guess or chase anyone."**

## Long-Term Brand Contribution

Guides are the ecosystem's most durable and most searched content: a well-written one
stays true and stays found for years, and it arrives at the exact moment someone needs
it. They also reduce real operational cost — cancellations, calls, wasted slots — which
makes this the rare campaign with a measurable internal return.

## Relationship With INSAN

**Trust platform: Continuity.**

A guide covers before, during and after — the continuity model made practical. Where a
sequence is identical at both hospitals, the guide quietly demonstrates one standard;
where it differs, saying so is more honest than implying uniformity.

# ═══════════ INSTITUTIONAL KNOWLEDGE ═══════════

## Institutional Knowledge Extensions

**This file deliberately contains no instructions.** Fasting durations, medication
rules, arrival times and preparation steps are clinical facts held by the services that
perform them, and they differ between hospitals and between procedures. A plausible
invented instruction here does not merely mislead — it can cause a cancellation or
clinical harm. **This campaign cannot run until the operator supplies verified
sequences.**

**The accompanying relative is an unserved reader worth taking seriously.** They do the
arranging, the driving and the remembering, and no hospital writes for them. A guide
addressed to them is unusual, immediately useful and shared.

**Guides age badly and remain findable.** A changed instruction requires an urgent
correction, because the old post continues to be found by search long after the practice
has changed. Whoever runs this campaign needs a review cycle, not just a publication
plan.
