# Purpose

This document is the Source of Truth for the **Meet Our Team** campaign.

It describes a campaign, not a medical entity. Where a medical service file answers
*what is this department*, this file answers *what is this campaign, what belongs
inside it, and what must never appear in it.*

Five scheduled posts depend on it.

# Document Philosophy

Written for a worker that has never seen INSAN and cannot ask. Everything here is
derived from `MASTER_BRAND_ARCHITECTURE.md`, `AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md`,
`PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md`, `PROJECT_DECISIONS.md` and the multidisciplinary
material in `departments/MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md`.

**Read the boundary section first.** This campaign sits between two others that look
like it, and the most common failure is not writing it badly — it is writing one of
the neighbours by accident.

# ═══════════ FOUNDATION ═══════════

## Overview

Meet Our Team introduces the people who deliver care and are not physicians: nurses,
technicians, coordinators, quality and infection-control staff, laboratory and
radiology teams, patient relations, reception, porters, housekeeping, security,
maintenance, administration.

It is the campaign that makes the phrase *"an entire team is caring for your
relative"* stop being a slogan and start being a list of named human beings with
jobs a reader can picture.

## Definition

A supporting campaign that features the **non-physician members** of the care system
in order to make the depth of the organisation visible.

**What is inside it:** who these people are, what their role actually involves during
a shift, the judgement it requires, how they connect to the rest of the team, and
what a patient or family would never know they did.

**What is outside it:**

- **Physicians.** They belong to *Meet Our Doctors*. A post about a doctor is that
  campaign even if a nurse appears in it.
- **Processes with no person at the centre.** Sterilisation cycles, stock control,
  equipment calibration, handover protocol — those are *Behind The Scenes*.
- **The atmosphere and rhythm of the institution** — the corridor at six in the
  morning, Ramadan on a ward, a birthday on a night shift. That is *Hospital Life*.
- Recruitment advertising. This is not a hiring campaign, even though it helps
  hiring.
- Clinical education delivered by a staff member. A nurse explaining wound care is
  *Educational* content that happens to feature a nurse.

### The routing test

Three supporting campaigns describe the same building and are easy to confuse. One
question separates them:

| If the post is fundamentally about… | It belongs to |
|---|---|
| **a person** — who they are, what they decide | **Meet Our Team** |
| **a process** — what happens that nobody sees | Behind The Scenes |
| **a place and its rhythm** — what it feels like here | Hospital Life |

A post can contain all three. File it by what would be lost if you removed it: take
the named person out of a Meet Our Team post and there is nothing left.

## Vision

That the public stops picturing a hospital as *doctors plus a building*, and starts
picturing it as an organisation of specialists — most of whom they have never thought
about, all of whom are why the care works.

## Mission

Give every role in the care system a face, a name and a reason to matter, so that
"multidisciplinary" becomes something the reader has actually met.

## Why This Service Exists

**The largest part of the care a patient receives is delivered by people they cannot
name.**

A patient remembers the surgeon. They do not remember the nurse who noticed their
breathing had changed at 3am, the technician who repeated a blood sample because the
first one looked wrong, the infection-control coordinator whose protocol is the
reason a wound did not become sepsis, or the porter who got them to imaging in time.

This has two consequences, and both are business problems:

**For patients**, a hospital is judged almost entirely on one or two visible
relationships. That makes quality feel like luck — you got a good doctor or you did
not — and it is precisely the perception `MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md` identifies as the
thing organised care exists to replace.

**For staff**, invisibility is corrosive. People who are never seen doing skilled
work eventually stop being treated as skilled. Retention, care quality and the
culture a physician judges when deciding where to work all follow from this.

This campaign closes both gaps with the same move: it shows the work.

It is also the direct expression of a strategic decision already taken. The ICU
knowledge base's *The ICU Army* section establishes that the patient is never treated
by one doctor alone and that this multidisciplinary collaboration is one of the
strongest differentiators available. That claim needs evidence, and this campaign is
where the evidence lives.

## Business Objectives

- Make organisational depth visible, which is what separates a managed institution
  from a building with doctors in it.
- Support recruitment and retention of clinical and support staff — a place that
  shows its people is a place people want to work.
- Strengthen the INSAN platform story: standards, training and coordination are
  things INSAN provides, and they are legible in the staff or nowhere.
- Reduce reliance on individual physician reputation as the sole trust asset.
- Support investor confidence by demonstrating that quality is systemic rather than
  personal.

## Marketing Objectives

- Move perception from *good doctors* to *a complete organisation*.
- Give the multidisciplinary claim concrete, memorable evidence.
- Build recognition for roles the audience has never considered.
- Humanise the institution without trivialising the work.
- Make internal audiences feel seen — staff are a real and intended audience here.

## Positioning

For a patient who assumes their care depends on getting a good doctor, Meet Our Team
shows the dozen other people whose judgement their outcome also rests on — where
competing campaigns feature staff as decorative background to a hospital's image,
this one treats each role as a discipline with its own expertise, and lets the reader
discover how much of their care they never knew about.

> *Test applied:* a competitor can photograph smiling nurses in a corridor. Explaining
> what a specific nurse is deciding in a specific moment requires actually knowing the
> work, and it cannot be copied by a campaign that treats staff as scenery.

## Brand Philosophy

**The system delivers the care. The people are the system.**

`AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md` §18 forbids selling doctors and requires selling
confidence in the medical team. This campaign is the constructive half of that rule:
it is not enough to avoid physician-celebrity messaging if nothing takes its place.

`PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md` §5 states *Human Before Technology* — the human is the
hero and equipment is an enabling tool. Here the human in question is usually the one
nobody photographs.

## Core Promise

**Every person shown here does the work described, in that role, with their consent —
and is presented as a professional exercising judgement, not as a friendly face.**

The promise is about the integrity of the portrayal. It says nothing about outcomes,
staffing levels or availability, because none of those can be promised.

# ═══════════ HUMAN UNDERSTANDING ═══════════

## Human Problem

What the patient or family is actually living through:

- *"I don't know who any of these people are or who to ask."*
- *"The doctor comes for five minutes. Who's watching the rest of the time?"*
- *"Is anyone actually paying attention, or am I just in a bed?"*
- *"I asked a nurse a question and I don't know if she's allowed to answer."*
- *"Everyone's rushing. I feel like I'm interrupting."*
- *"If something goes wrong at night, is there anyone here who knows what to do?"*
- *"They keep saying 'the team'. Which team? I've seen four different people."*

Beneath these: **the family cannot see the organisation, so they assume there is not
one.** Uniforms without names read as interchangeable, and interchangeable reads as
nobody in particular being responsible.

For staff, a different problem entirely: **being defined by their uniform.** A nurse
with fifteen years of critical-care experience is addressed as "sister" by people who
would address a first-year resident as "doctor". Skilled work performed invisibly
gets categorised as unskilled.

## Human Insight

**Families judge a hospital by the people they were not supposed to notice.**

They cannot evaluate the surgery. They can evaluate whether the person taking blood
explained what they were doing, whether the cleaner apologised for the noise, whether
anyone made eye contact at 4am. And they are right to: those behaviours are the most
reliable signal of institutional culture available to an outsider, because they are
the ones nobody thought to perform.

The uncomfortable corollary is that the reverse is also true and is how reputations
are actually lost. A hospital does not get a bad name because a surgery failed —
failure is understood to be possible. It gets one because someone was rude at
reception while a family was frightened.

> Would a patient read it and think *"how did they know that?"* — yes. Every family
> that has spent a night in a hospital has formed their entire opinion of the
> institution from a handful of interactions with people whose names they never
> learned, and nobody has ever said so out loud in a hospital's own advertising.

## Invisible Product

The visible product is an introduction to a member of staff.

What the family is really buying:

- **Someone is watching when the doctor is not.** Continuity of attention.
- **Names to say.** A family that can say *"ask Mona"* has stopped being lost.
- **Permission to speak.** Knowing a role exists is knowing who to ask.
- **Evidence that competence is distributed** — so their outcome does not hang on
  one person being present, awake and correct.
- **Relief from vigilance.** Families exhaust themselves trying to supervise care
  they do not understand. Seeing the organisation lets them stop.

That last one is the real product. The family that trusts the system sleeps.

## Psychological Transformation

**Before:**
> *"There's a doctor, and then there's a lot of people in uniforms. I'm watching
> everything myself because I don't know who's responsible for what."*

**After:**
> *"There's a nurse who owns this shift, a coordinator I can ask, and a lab that
> re-checks things. They know what they're doing and they know what each other are
> doing. I can stop guarding."*

The movement is from **surveillance to trust** — from a family policing care they
cannot assess, to a family that can rest because they have seen the shape of the
organisation holding it.

## Emotional Strategy

**Primary emotion: reassurance** — the specific kind that comes from discovering
there was more support present than you realised.

Supporting: respect, recognition, warmth, quiet pride, relief.

Never: pity, heroism, sentimentality, or the "angels in scrubs" register. Calling
staff heroes is a way of not paying attention to what they actually do, and
`AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md` §8 forbids exaggeration and emotional manipulation
outright.

The register is professional warmth. These are skilled people doing demanding work
competently — not saints, and not the grateful poor.

## Audience

**Primary — patients and families, current and future.**
People who have been in a hospital or expect to be. They are trying to work out
whether care here is organised or improvised.

**Secondary — current staff, across both hospitals.**
An unusually important secondary audience. This campaign is read internally as a
statement about whether the organisation values non-physician work, and that
perception affects retention, morale and how the ecosystem is described by the people
inside it.

**Secondary — prospective staff.**
Nurses and technicians choose employers partly on how they expect to be treated. This
campaign is the most honest recruitment material the ecosystem has, precisely because
it is not recruitment material.

**Supporting — physicians.**
30% of audience priority under the Constitution. A doctor reads this campaign as
evidence of the quality of the team they would be working with, which is one of the
strongest factors in where a clinician chooses to practise.

**Supporting — investors and partners.**
Reading operational depth as a durable asset rather than a personality-dependent one.

**Who decides, who influences, who pays:** the patient or an adult child decides where
to attend; the family member who spent the night influences most; the patient or an
insurer pays.

## Audience Decision

**What they compare:** how they were treated last time, what a relative reported,
whether the place felt organised or chaotic on a first visit.

**What they fear:** being left alone at night; nobody noticing a change in condition;
having to fight for basic information; being a burden for asking.

**Where they look:** their own memory of a previous visit first, then relatives, then
social media.

**What actually moves them:** a concrete detail of attentiveness. *"The nurse checks
the drip and the patient's face, because the face changes first"* does more than any
claim about staffing ratios — it is evidence of a way of working, and the reader can
verify it the next time they are in a ward.

# ═══════════ MEDICAL FOUNDATION ═══════════

## Service Boundaries

- This campaign does **not** feature physicians as its subject.
- It does **not** state staffing numbers, ratios or shift coverage. Those are
  operational facts nobody has committed to publicly, and a published ratio becomes a
  promise about every shift.
- It does **not** give clinical advice, even when the person featured is qualified to.
- It does **not** feature any staff member without documented consent.
- It does **not** discuss identifiable patients, or show them without consent.
- It does **not** rank staff, name a "best" nurse, or run staff-of-the-month content
  publicly. Internal recognition is not a campaign.
- It does **not** imply that a specific person will be present for a specific patient.

## Medical Philosophy

Care is delivered by a coordinated team in which no individual owns the outcome.
`MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md` states this as a principle — *no single physician, no single
nurse, no single machine delivers critical care alone; the system delivers it* — and
this campaign is its public form.

The philosophy has a communication consequence that is easy to get wrong: the point of
featuring an individual is to demonstrate the system. A post that makes one nurse
indispensable has argued against the thing it was supposed to prove.

## Core Principles

**Show the judgement, not the task.** Anyone can be photographed carrying a tray. The
content is what they were deciding while they carried it.

**One person, one post.** Group content dilutes into scenery. The Constitution's visual
philosophy (§11) forbids crowded compositions for the same reason.

**Never without consent.** Documented, specific, and revocable. A staff member who
regrets a post must be able to have it removed, and knowing that is what makes the
next colleague agree.

**No role is a supporting role.** Housekeeping in an operating theatre is infection
control. Reception is the first clinical triage a patient meets. If a role is included
at all, it is included as expertise.

**Accuracy over polish.** A nurse describing her work in her own words, imperfectly,
is worth more than a scripted line. Staff can tell instantly when a colleague has been
put in a costume, and so can patients.

**Protect the person from the campaign.** The featured individual carries the
consequences — patients recognising them, colleagues commenting, family sharing it.
The campaign's needs never outweigh their comfort.

## Core Features

The roles available to this campaign, each with what makes it substantive. This list
is the campaign's raw material, drawn from the multidisciplinary structure recorded in
`MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md` and the operational structure in `PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md`.

**Nursing**
- Head nurse — owns the shift, the allocation and the escalation decision.
- Critical-care nursing — continuous observation; the discipline of noticing a change
  before a monitor alarms.
- Ward nursing — medication safety, wound care, mobilisation, the patient's day.
- Assistant nursing staff — the hands-on continuity a patient meets most often.
- The core judgement across all of them: **when to escalate.** A nurse's most
  consequential decision is deciding that something is wrong before it is obvious.

**Quality and safety**
- Quality coordinator — audits practice against protocol, and closes the gap.
- Infection-control coordinator — surveillance, protocol, and the authority to stop a
  procedure. The most underrated clinical role in any hospital.
- Occupational safety coordinator — staff protection, which is patient protection.

**Diagnostics**
- Laboratory team and laboratory manager — sample integrity, turnaround, and the
  judgement to repeat a result that does not fit the patient.
- Radiology team — positioning, protocol selection, radiation dose, and recognising an
  image that needs a radiologist now rather than in the morning.

**Coordination and experience**
- Patient relations / Family Care Coordinator — the role
  `MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md` establishes as a deliberate evolution from traditional PR:
  a human guide through the process, not a complaints desk.
- Administrative coordination — admissions, insurance, discharge paperwork; the
  friction a family feels most acutely during a crisis.
- Reception — the first person a frightened family meets, and the one who sets their
  expectation of the whole institution.

**Support services**
- Housekeeping — in a clinical environment this is infection control performed at
  scale, every day, invisibly.
- Porters and transport — time-critical movement between departments.
- Maintenance and biomedical engineering — the reason equipment works when it is
  needed.
- Security — de-escalation, access control, and the calm presence in an emergency
  department at 2am.
- Catering and nutrition — therapeutic diets are treatment.

**Medical administration**
- Operational supervision and continuous review, under the INSAN standard.

## Differentiators

What is genuinely different here, versus how staff appear in this market's healthcare
marketing:

**Roles are treated as disciplines, not as atmosphere.** The market standard is staff
as background texture in an institutional video. Here each role is the subject, with
its own expertise stated.

**The support functions are included at all.** Almost nobody features housekeeping,
maintenance or security. Including them is both accurate — they are why the clinical
work is possible — and immediately distinctive.

**Judgement is the content.** Not *what does this person do* but *what do they decide,
and how do they know*. This is the same mechanism *Meet Our Doctors* uses, applied
where it has never been applied.

**It reinforces the platform rather than the site.** Standards, training and
coordination come from INSAN; a team that visibly works to one standard across two
hospitals is the platform's proof, not the hospital's.

**It is one campaign across two hospitals.** A nurse at Future and a nurse at Delta
demonstrating the same standard is an argument no single-hospital competitor can make.

## Service Journey

How a family actually encounters the team, in order — and therefore the sequence the
campaign can follow:

1. **Security and reception** — the first two people met, usually while frightened.
2. **Triage or admissions** — the first assessment, and the first sense of whether
   this place is organised.
3. **Administrative coordination** — insurance, paperwork, cost. Where trust is most
   often lost.
4. **Ward or unit nursing** — the relationship that lasts the whole stay.
5. **Diagnostics** — laboratory and radiology, mostly invisible to the family.
6. **Patient relations / Family Care Coordinator** — updates, explanation, escalation.
7. **Housekeeping and catering** — daily contact, constant, rarely acknowledged.
8. **Discharge coordination** — instructions, follow-up, the handover home.

Each step is a post. The sequence is also an argument: by the end, the reader has met
the organisation.

## What Makes This Different

For a worker reading nothing else: **feature one named non-physician, show the
judgement their role requires, and connect them to the team around them.** Not a
portrait, not a thank-you, not a hero. A professional, doing skilled work, inside a
system.

# ═══════════ MARKETING INTELLIGENCE ═══════════

## Communication Objective

To make the organisation visible.

Not to praise staff — praise is cheap and reads as internal. The objective is that a
reader finishes a post knowing something specific about how care is delivered that
they did not know before, and knowing it because they met a person.

## What We Are Really Selling

**Depth.**

The visible content is a member of staff. What is being sold is the discovery that
the patient's outcome does not rest on one relationship — that there are a dozen
people with distinct expertise between them and a bad day, most of whom they will
never meet.

Restated for a worker: we are not selling *nice staff*. We are selling **a
distributed, coordinated competence** that makes care predictable rather than lucky.

## Human Truth

**People trust institutions they can picture.**

An abstraction cannot be trusted, only accepted or doubted. The moment an
organisation becomes a set of specific people doing specific things, it becomes
something a person can form a real relationship with — and the relationship survives
any individual leaving.

The second truth: **being seen changes how people work.** A staff member whose skill
has been publicly recognised behaves like someone whose skill matters. That is not a
marketing effect; it is the campaign paying for itself internally.

## Audience Psychology

How the audience thinks about hospital staff before anything is said:

- **Doctors are the hospital; everyone else is staff.** A deeply held and rarely
  examined hierarchy.
- **Nurses are kind, not skilled.** Warmth is attributed generously and expertise is
  not attributed at all.
- **Support roles are not part of care.** Cleaning, security and catering are read as
  building services rather than clinical safety.
- **Everyone is overworked, so nobody is really paying attention.** A defensive
  assumption formed from experience elsewhere.
- **Complaining will make things worse.** Which is why families watch instead of
  asking.

Each of these is a belief the campaign can correct by demonstration, and none of them
can be corrected by assertion.

## Emotional Triggers

**Primary:** reassurance.

**Supporting:** respect, recognition, warmth, gratitude, quiet pride, relief.

Deliberately excluded: pity, awe, sentimentality, indignation about how hard the work
is. The last one is a trap — it feels supportive and it reinforces the belief that the
system is under-resourced and inattentive.

## Psychological Barriers

**Barrier 1 — "They're just staff."**
The hierarchy that categorises non-physician work as unskilled. Corrected only by
showing judgement: a decision, its stakes, and how the person knew.

**Barrier 2 — "This is a photo opportunity."**
Audiences are practised at recognising staged corporate warmth and discount it
entirely. Corrected by specificity — a real shift, a real decision, the person's own
words.

**Barrier 3 — "Nice people, but is the care any good?"**
Warmth without competence reads as compensation for weak medicine. Every post must
carry a competence signal, not only a human one.

**Barrier 4 — "Everyone's too busy to help me."**
The belief that stops families asking questions, and therefore the belief that most
damages their experience. Corrected by showing that answering is part of the job, not
an interruption to it.

**Barrier 5 — "They only show you the good parts."**
Cynicism about institutional communication generally. Corrected by including the
unglamorous roles and the difficult moments — a campaign that features housekeeping is
harder to dismiss as a highlight reel.

## Core Messaging Framework

Consistently reinforced:

- The patient is cared for by an organisation, not by an individual.
- Every role in that organisation requires judgement.
- The people who are least visible are often the most consequential.
- Standards are shared across both hospitals because INSAN sets them.
- A team that knows what each other are doing is what makes care predictable.
- Being able to name who to ask is part of good care.

## Messaging Hierarchy

1. **A person, doing a specific thing, at a specific moment.**
2. **The judgement inside it** — what they were deciding, and how they knew.
3. **The team around them** — who they hand to, who they escalate to.
4. **The standard** — that this is how it works here, in both hospitals.
5. **INSAN** — the platform that sets and maintains the standard.

Never reverse it. A post that opens with the standard has written a policy statement
with a photograph attached.

If there is only one line, it is the first: the person and the moment.

## Narrative Themes

Recurring stories with enough material for months:

- **The escalation.** A nurse decides something is wrong before it is obvious, and
  says so.
- **The repeat.** A laboratory technician re-runs a sample because the result does not
  match the patient in front of them.
- **The handover.** A shift ends and the knowledge does not — what gets transferred,
  and what would happen if it were not.
- **The first face.** Reception or security meeting a family at their worst moment.
- **The protocol that prevented something.** Infection control, told as a near-miss
  that never became one.
- **The invisible clock.** A porter, a sample, a scan — the work that is only visible
  when it is late.
- **The translator.** A coordinator turning a clinical explanation into something a
  family can act on.
- **The night.** What the organisation looks like at 3am, and who is holding it.
- **The person behind the role.** Why this individual chose this work, and what they
  find difficult about it.
- **The same standard, two hospitals.** The same role at Future and at Delta, working
  to one protocol.
- **The colleague's account.** A physician describing what a nurse caught — trust
  transferred across the hierarchy the audience believes in.
- **The thing you never knew happened.** Any part of the patient's own care they were
  present for and did not perceive.

## Content Pillars

**1. The Judgement** — the decision each role owns, and how it is made.
**2. The Invisible Work** — care delivered outside the patient's awareness.
**3. The Chain** — how roles hand to each other, and what the handover protects.
**4. The Person** — who they are, why this work, in their own words.
**5. The Standard** — what INSAN requires of every role, in both hospitals.
**6. The Ask** — who to speak to about what, and the fact that asking is welcome.

## Storytelling Opportunities

Moments, not topics — each one filmable, and each one carrying a decision:

- A nurse's eyes moving from the monitor to the patient's face, because the face
  changes first.
- The handover conversation at the end of a shift: what is said, and what is written.
- A laboratory technician holding a result up against the request form and deciding to
  run it again.
- The infection-control coordinator stopping in a doorway and pointing at something.
- Reception recognising a family who came last month, and asking after the patient by
  name.
- A radiographer repositioning a patient by two centimetres and explaining why.
- The porter checking the corridor ahead before moving a bed.
- Housekeeping working around a sleeping patient without waking them.
- A Family Care Coordinator sitting down — not standing — to deliver an update.
- Security walking beside a distressed relative rather than in front of them.
- The night nurse's torch, low, checking without disturbing.
- The biomedical engineer testing a machine nobody is currently using.
- Two nurses at different hospitals following the same checklist, in the same order.
- Catering checking a therapeutic diet against a chart before the tray leaves.

## Communication Style

Always: human, specific, respectful, calm, concrete, quietly confident.

Never: sentimental, congratulatory, heroic, pitying, corporate, or written in the
voice of management describing its own staff.

The voice is closer to good documentary than to advertising. The reader should feel
they were shown something, not told something.

## Tone of Voice

Write as someone who spent a shift watching and found it more interesting than they
expected. Not as an employer praising employees — that register makes staff
uncomfortable and readers suspicious, and it turns a person into a company asset.

Let the person's own words carry the post wherever possible. Edit for clarity, never
for polish; an over-smooth quotation is the fastest way to lose the reader's belief.

## Messaging Rules

- Always name the person and their role.
- Always show a decision, not only an activity.
- Always connect them to the team — who they hand to, who they escalate to.
- Always obtain and record consent before writing.
- Always keep the individual subordinate to the standard: if this person left, the
  post must still be true of the organisation.
- Never call staff heroes, angels, warriors or a family.
- Never state staffing numbers, ratios or coverage.
- Never imply a named person will be available to a specific patient.
- Never show an identifiable patient without consent.
- Never let a support role be framed as lesser.
- Never use a staff member's story to make a claim about outcomes.
- Never publish a post a colleague would find embarrassing.

# ═══════════ OFFERS ═══════════

## Current Offers

**No offers.** This campaign advertises nothing and asks for nothing to be purchased.
An offer attached to a staff profile turns a person into a sales instrument, which is
the exact failure `AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md` §21 forbids.

## Offer Rules

- No promotional offer may be attached to a post featuring a member of staff.
- No pricing, discount or package may appear in this campaign under any circumstances.
- Recruitment openings are not offers and do not belong here either; a hiring post is
  a different campaign with a different consent conversation.

## Call To Action

In priority order, and all of them soft:

1. **None.** Most posts in this campaign should ask for nothing. The action is that
   the reader now knows the organisation is there.
2. **Ask when you are here.** Encourage patients to speak to the role shown — it
   converts the post into better care.
3. **Follow the page** for more of the ecosystem's work.
4. **Learn about the service** the person works in, where one exists.

Never a booking CTA on a staff post. The mismatch between a human portrait and a
commercial ask is exactly the register the Constitution rules out.

## Primary KPI

**Engagement quality — comments that name a role or a person, and shares.**

Chosen deliberately over reach. A post that reaches 50,000 people and produces nothing
has not changed a belief; a post that produces twenty comments naming a nurse someone
remembers has. Staff sentiment is the secondary measure and is read internally.

# ═══════════ STRATEGIC GOVERNANCE ═══════════

## Strategic Reminder

Before publishing, ask what the reader believes afterwards.

If the answer is *"that hospital has nice staff"*, the post failed. If it is *"there
are more people thinking about a patient than I realised, and they know what they are
doing"*, it worked.

## Strategic Constraints

- The campaign never competes by comparison with other hospitals' staff.
- It never uses staff hardship to generate sympathy.
- It never makes a clinical claim through a staff member's mouth that the ecosystem
  could not make directly.
- Professional dignity always outranks campaign performance. A post that would perform
  well and embarrass its subject does not run.
- Consent is revocable. If a featured person later asks for removal, the post comes
  down without negotiation.
- Long-term brand value outranks short-term engagement — `AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md`
  §20.5.

## Medical Communication Principles

- Nothing in this campaign constitutes medical advice, regardless of the speaker's
  qualifications.
- A staff member describing their work must not describe a patient's case.
- Clinical uncertainty is respected: a role may be shown deciding, and the decision
  may be shown as difficult.
- Every statement must be supportable. If a role's described responsibility is not
  actually how it works here, it is a fabrication about a real institution.

## Never Promise

The campaign must never promise:

- That a specific person will be present, on duty, or assigned to a patient.
- Any staffing ratio, response time or coverage level.
- That a member of staff will be available to answer a particular question.
- Any clinical outcome, improvement or prevention attributable to the team.
- That the team is the best, most qualified or most experienced in the market.
- Continuity of a named individual across a patient's stay.
- Anything about another institution's staff.
- That every shift looks like the one shown.

## Can Promise

The campaign can confidently state:

- That these roles exist and carry these responsibilities in both hospitals.
- That care is delivered by a coordinated multidisciplinary team.
- That INSAN sets and maintains a shared standard across the ecosystem
  (`MASTER_BRAND_ARCHITECTURE.md` §3).
- That families are treated as partners and questions are welcome
  (`PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md` §3).
- That the person shown consented to appear and does the work described.
- That handover, escalation and infection-control protocols are part of how care runs.

Each of these is either an organisational fact recorded in a committed document, or a
statement about the portrayal itself.

## Brand Perception

After sustained exposure, the audience should believe:

**"That is an organisation, not a building with doctors in it. If I go there, I am
handed to a system — and the people in it are treated like professionals, which is
probably why they behave like professionals."**

Staff should believe: *"They showed the work properly. They did not make us into a
poster."*

## Long-Term Brand Contribution

This campaign builds the asset that survives staff turnover.

A brand carried by individual physicians is fragile: it leaves when they do. A brand
carried by a demonstrated organisational standard is durable, transferable to a third
hospital, and is precisely what makes INSAN a platform rather than a holding company.

It is also the most credible internal communication the ecosystem produces. What is
said publicly about how staff are valued becomes what staff expect — which, over
time, makes it true.

## Relationship With INSAN

**Trust platform: Governance.**

The team is where INSAN's standard becomes observable. Hospitals employ the people;
INSAN sets what is required of every role, trains to it, and audits it — the
distinction `MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md` states as permanent: *the hospitals deliver the
service, INSAN continuously develops the system behind the service.*

Every post reinforces this without stating it as a slogan: the same role, the same
protocol, the same standard at Future and at Delta. That is what a platform looks like
from the outside, and it is invisible in any campaign that features only doctors.

Cross-platform reinforcement per `AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md` §16: a post on a
hospital page reinforces that the hospital operates under the INSAN standard; a post
on the INSAN page reinforces that the standard reaches into both hospitals. Never
forced, never at the cost of the story.

## Institutional Knowledge Extensions

**Why this campaign is scheduled at five slots.** It carries a disproportionate share
of the ecosystem's evidence for its central claim — that care here is systemic. Five
posts is enough to establish a pattern and not enough to exhaust it, and the pattern
is the point: a single staff post reads as a gesture, a sequence reads as a
disclosure.

**The consent question is not administrative.** In this market, staff are frequently
photographed for institutional material without meaningful consent, and the first
person to be asked properly will be surprised. Treat that surprise as a signal of how
much trust this campaign can build internally if it is run well — and how much it will
destroy if it is not.

**The three-campaign confusion is the known failure mode.** Meet Our Team, Behind The
Scenes and Hospital Life were scheduled as separate campaigns and will collapse into
one another unless the routing test in *Definition* is applied every time. The audit
finding that 88% of finished posts share five opening formulas came from exactly this
kind of unnoticed convergence.

**The Family Care Coordinator role is a strategic decision, not a job title.**
`MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md` records the deliberate evolution of traditional patient
relations into a guide for families. Where this campaign features that role, it is
demonstrating an organisational choice INSAN made — which is more valuable than the
individual.

**What is not yet known.** Actual role titles, reporting lines and department
structures at each hospital are operational facts held by the operator. This file
describes the roles that a hospital of this type contains and that the committed
documents reference; before a specific person is featured, their real title and
responsibilities must be confirmed rather than assumed from this list.

## AI Worker Guidance

**Apply the routing test before writing.** If the post's subject is a process or an
atmosphere rather than a person, it belongs to a different campaign.

**Common mistakes with this entity:**

- Writing warmth instead of competence. Every post needs both; only one of them is
  ever missing.
- Making the individual the point. If the post stops being true when that person
  leaves, rewrite it.

  decision, not from numbers.

  until it is a list of people with jobs.
- Reaching for heroism. It is the laziest available register and it is forbidden.
