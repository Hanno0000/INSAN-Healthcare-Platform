# ═══════════ FOUNDATION ═══════════

## Overview

Health Awareness explains a condition, a body system or a health concept to people who
do not know about it — before they need to.

Its reader is not wrong about anything and is not being asked to do anything. They
simply do not know, and the campaign's job is to make a subject thinkable before it
becomes urgent.

## Definition

An educational campaign that builds understanding of health conditions and concepts,
without correcting a belief and without asking for an action.

**What is inside it:** what a condition is and how it behaves; what a symptom means in
general terms; how a body system works, where it helps; what a test measures; what
happens in a treatment; the vocabulary people need in order to understand their own
care.

**What is outside it:**

- **A wrong belief.** *Medical Myths & Facts*.
- **Advice to act.** *Prevention Tips*.
- **Seasonal or occasion-linked relevance.** *Seasonal Campaigns*.
- **Practical questions about using the ecosystem.** *FAQ*.
- **Anything about an individual's case.** Never.

### The educational routing test

| If the reader's state is… | It belongs to |
|---|---|
| they believe something that is wrong | Medical Myths & Facts |
| **they do not know something** | **Health Awareness** |
| they know but do not act | Prevention Tips |
| the moment makes it relevant now | Seasonal Campaigns |

## Vision

That people arrive at their own care with enough vocabulary to understand it — which is
the difference between being treated and participating in treatment.

## Mission

Make a subject thinkable before it is urgent, without asking the reader to admit they
are at risk.

## Why This Service Exists

**People understand their own care badly, and the cost falls on them.**

A patient who does not know what their diagnosis means cannot weigh options, cannot
recognise deterioration, cannot follow instructions they did not understand and cannot
ask the question that would have helped. `MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md` records that families
rarely understand medical detail — and that translating complexity into something they
can act on is part of the care system rather than an add-on.

Doing that translation publicly, before anyone is a patient, is cheaper and reaches
people who will never ask.

It is also the ecosystem's stated position.
`PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md` §5 lists *Education Before Promotion* as a permanent
strategic differentiator: build understanding first, and do not ask for the booking.

## Business Objectives

- Build understanding that makes patients easier to treat and safer to discharge.
- Reach people who avoid healthcare content, through interest rather than urgency.
- Establish medical authority without asserting it.
- Support earlier presentation, which is the largest available clinical gain in this
  market.

## Marketing Objectives

- Explain one thing clearly per post.
- Never require the reader to identify as at risk.
- Build the vocabulary patients need in order to ask good questions.
- Ask for nothing.

## Positioning

For anyone who has been given a diagnosis, a test result or a word they did not
understand and did not want to ask about, Health Awareness explains it plainly — where
competing health content alarms or advises, this one simply explains, and asks for
nothing.

> *Test applied:* a competitor can publish an awareness-day graphic. Explaining what a
> test actually measures, in language a person can repeat to a relative, requires
> someone to have done the translation.

## Brand Philosophy

**Education before promotion** (`PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md` §5), and **never through
fear** (`AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md` §21).

Both bite hard here. Awareness content competes for attention against fear content and
loses on engagement — and the correct response is to accept the lower engagement rather
than to reach for the fear.

## Core Promise

**Everything here is medically accurate, clinically reviewed, explained rather than
asserted, and asks nothing of you.**

# ═══════════ HUMAN UNDERSTANDING ═══════════

## Human Problem

- *"The doctor said a word and I nodded."*
- *"I don't know what my father's diagnosis actually means."*
- *"I looked it up and now I think I'm dying."*
- *"I don't understand what the test was for."*
- *"I didn't want to ask him to explain again."*
- *"I'd rather not know."*

Beneath these: **people are managing conditions they do not understand**, and the
information environment available to them — search results and relatives — is worse than
nothing.

## Human Insight

**People avoid learning about the thing they are most at risk from, and the avoidance is
protective.**

A man with a family history of heart disease does not read about heart disease. A woman
who has found a lump does not search for what it might be. Not knowing preserves the
possibility that everything is fine, and that possibility is doing real psychological
work.

This is the exact opposite of what awareness campaigns assume. They are written as
though the barrier were access to information, and they address the reader as someone at
risk — *"do you know the signs of…"* — which is precisely the frame the person most at
risk is avoiding.

> Would a reader think *"how did they know that?"* — yes. Everyone has decided not to
> look something up, and no health campaign has ever been written as though that were
> a reasonable thing to do.

The operating consequence: **never require the reader to identify as at risk in order to
engage.** Write about the subject, not about them. *"What blood pressure actually
measures"* is readable by everyone. *"Are you at risk of hypertension?"* is readable only
by people who have already accepted that they might be — who are the people who least
need it.

## Invisible Product

- **Vocabulary** — the words needed to understand and to ask.
- **Permission to look**, granted by content that does not accuse.
- **Correction of a frightening search result.**
- **The ability to explain it to a relative.**
- **Participation** — being a party to a decision rather than a subject of it.

## Psychological Transformation

**Before:** *"I nodded and left. I do not really know what is happening, and looking it
up makes it worse."*

**After:** *"I understand roughly what this is. I know what to ask next time."*

From **being treated to participating.**

## Emotional Strategy

**Primary: curiosity** — the campaign's only sustainable engine, and the one that does
not require the reader to feel threatened.

Supporting: relief, competence, calm interest.

Never: fear, urgency, alarm, statistics about mortality, or the *"do you know the signs"*
framing. Fear is banned by §21 and it also selects for the wrong audience.

## Audience

**Primary — people who are not currently patients.** The whole point is to reach them
before.

**Primary — family members managing someone else's condition.** Usually the household's
health decision-maker, usually reading on someone else's behalf, and unusually motivated
to understand.

**Secondary — recently diagnosed patients** trying to understand what they were told.

**Secondary — people frightened by a search result.**

**Supporting — physicians**, who judge medical accuracy and will notice a single error.

## Audience Decision

**What they fear:** learning something they cannot unlearn; being told to act.

**What actually moves them:** an explanation that is interesting on its own. Health
content that would be worth reading even if it did not apply to you is the version that
reaches the people it applies to.

# ═══════════ MEDICAL FOUNDATION ═══════════

## Service Boundaries

- This campaign does **not** diagnose, assess symptoms or advise anyone whether to seek
  care for their own situation.
- It does **not** recommend, name or discourage a medication, dose or brand.
- It does **not** address contested or evolving clinical questions.
- It does **not** use fear, mortality statistics or worst-case framing.
- It does **not** publish a figure or study finding the operator has not approved.
- It does **not** promote a service, screening or test the ecosystem offers.
- It does **not** replace consultation, and says so.

## Medical Philosophy

Health literacy is part of care. A patient who understands their condition adheres
better, recognises deterioration earlier and participates in decisions — all of which
`MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md` treats as care outcomes rather than communication ones.

**Clinical review is mandatory for every post**, for the same reason it is mandatory in
*Medical Myths & Facts*: authoritative content that is nearly right is more dangerous
than none, and it travels.

## Core Principles

**Never require the reader to be at risk.** The subject is the subject, not the reader.

**Explain, do not alarm.**

**One concept per post.** Comprehension is the product.

**Clinical review, every post.**

**Settled medicine only.**

**Always end at the individual boundary** — this is general, your case needs a clinician.

**Never promote.** An awareness post that arrives at a service is an advertisement, and
the campaign's authority depends on not being one.

## Core Features

The territories available. All specific content requires clinical authorship and review.

- **What a condition is** — mechanism, plainly, without prognosis.
- **What a test measures** — blood pressure, blood sugar, cholesterol, imaging.
- **What a result means in general** — ranges and what they indicate, never applied to a
  reader.
- **How a body system works**, where understanding it changes comprehension of care.
- **What a treatment does** — the mechanism, not the recommendation.
- **The vocabulary of care** — the words used in a consultation, translated.
- **What a specialty actually treats** — which is also how people find the right route.
- **What "chronic" means** — the concept underneath most long-term care, and widely
  misunderstood.

## Differentiators

**It does not address the reader as at risk.** The category norm, and the reason
awareness content fails to reach the people it is for.

**It asks for nothing.** No screening, no booking, no service.

**It is clinically reviewed.** Most health content in this market is not.

**It explains mechanisms rather than listing symptoms.** Symptom lists produce anxiety;
mechanisms produce understanding.

## What Makes This Different

For a worker reading nothing else: **explain one thing about the body or about medicine
clearly, without addressing the reader as a patient, and ask for nothing.**

# ═══════════ MARKETING INTELLIGENCE ═══════════

## Communication Objective

To make one thing understood.

The test: could the reader explain it to somebody else afterwards?

## What We Are Really Selling

**Comprehension.**

The visible content is an explanation. What is being sold is the ability to participate
in your own care — and, for the ecosystem, the position of being the place where things
are explained properly.

## Human Truth

**People can hold frightening information much better than they can hold confusion.**

Uncertainty is the harder state. An explanation that is calm and complete reduces
anxiety even when its content is serious — which is why explanation, not reassurance, is
the correct instrument.

## Audience Psychology

- **Searching makes it worse.** Almost universally true.
- **I would rather not know.** Protective avoidance.
- **Health content is trying to frighten me into something.** Which makes the absence of
  an ask disproportionately persuasive.
- **Doctors do not have time to explain.** Often true, and the gap this fills.

## Emotional Triggers

**Primary:** curiosity. **Supporting:** relief, competence, calm.
Excluded: fear, urgency, alarm, guilt.

## Psychological Barriers

**Barrier 1 — "I would rather not know."** Corrected by never framing the post around
risk to the reader.
**Barrier 2 — "This will frighten me."** Corrected by mechanism over symptom list, and
by tone.
**Barrier 3 — "They want me to book something."** Corrected by never asking.
**Barrier 4 — "It will be too technical."** Corrected by explaining purpose before
mechanism, and by using no term without translating it.
**Barrier 5 — "Everything causes everything."** Awareness fatigue. Corrected by choosing
subjects where understanding changes comprehension, not by covering more ground.

## Core Messaging Framework

- Here is what this actually is.
- Here is what it means when a doctor says it.
- Here is what the test measures.
- This is general; your own case needs a clinician.
- We are not asking you to do anything.

## Messaging Hierarchy

1. **The thing being explained**, named plainly.
2. **Why it matters** in general terms — not to the reader personally.
3. **The explanation**, with the mechanism.
4. **The vocabulary** a reader can now use.
5. **The boundary** — general, not personal.

Never open by addressing the reader as at risk. If there is only one line, it is the
plain statement of what is being explained.

## Narrative Themes

- **The word the doctor used.** Translation as the frame.
- **What the test is actually measuring.**
- **What "chronic" means.**
- **How this part of the body works**, told as something interesting.
- **What the specialty actually treats** — which also routes people correctly.
- **What a result range means**, generally and carefully.
- **Why searching makes it worse** — an honest post about the information environment.

## Content Pillars

**1. What The Words Mean** · **2. What The Test Measures** · **3. How It Works** ·
**4. What Happens In Treatment** · **5. Which Specialty Treats What** ·
**6. Understanding A Result**

## Storytelling Opportunities

- A consultation sentence, translated line by line.
- A blood pressure reading explained as two numbers and what each one is.
- A body system explained as a mechanism, with no reference to disease.
- A test tube, and the journey of what it measures.
- The difference between two specialties people routinely confuse.
- An honest explanation of why search results frighten people.

## Communication Style

Always: plain, curious, calm, complete, unhurried.

Never: alarming, urgent, technical without translation, or addressed to a patient.

## Tone of Voice

Write as a doctor explaining something to a friend over tea, who is interested in being
understood rather than in sounding expert.

Never use: *silent killer*, *do you know the signs*, *could you be at risk*, *shocking*,
*what your doctor is not telling you*. Every one is the fear register §21 forbids.

## Messaging Rules

- Always explain the mechanism.
- Always translate every technical term.
- Always end at the individual boundary.
- Always have a clinician review it.
- Never address the reader as at risk.
- Never use fear, mortality figures or urgency.
- Never name a medication.
- Never promote a service, test or screening.
- Never cover a contested clinical question.
- Never publish an unapproved figure.

# ═══════════ STRATEGIC GOVERNANCE ═══════════

## Strategic Reminder

Before publishing, ask: **is the reader less confused, and no more frightened?**

Both must be true. An accurate post that raises anxiety has failed and has taught the
reader to avoid this source.

## Strategic Constraints

- Never use fear.
- Never promote a service.
- Never publish without clinical review.
- Never address a contested clinical question.
- Clinical accuracy outranks campaign performance absolutely.
- Long-term brand value outranks short-term engagement — and this campaign will always
  underperform fear-based health content, which is the correct trade.

## Medical Communication Principles

- Every post clinically reviewed. No exceptions.
- Settled medicine only.
- No diagnosis, symptom assessment or advice on seeking care.
- No medication named.
- Every post ends at the individual boundary.
- Where the honest answer is conditional, say so.

## Never Promise

- That understanding prevents, protects against or cures anything.
- That the information applies to the reader's case.
- Any outcome from knowing.
- Any statistic or study finding not approved.
- That medical understanding will not change.

## Can Promise

- That the explanation is accurate, settled and clinically reviewed.
- That it asks for nothing.
- That education precedes promotion here (`PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md` §5).
- That a question about a specific person needs a clinician.

## Brand Perception

**"They explain things properly, they don't try to frighten me, and they don't want
anything. That's where I go to understand something."**

## Long-Term Brand Contribution

This campaign builds authority without asserting it, reaches an audience no promotional
campaign can, and produces the ecosystem's most durable content — an explanation stays
true and stays searched for years.

Its deepest contribution is a habit: being the place people go to understand something.
That habit cannot be bought and compounds indefinitely.

## Relationship With INSAN

**Trust platform: Expertise.**

A shared standard for what may be published as medical fact, applied identically across
all three pages, is a platform function. This campaign should carry the lightest
branding of any in the plan: an explanation wearing a logo reads as an advertisement,
and asking for nothing is its whole credibility.

# ═══════════ INSTITUTIONAL KNOWLEDGE ═══════════

## Institutional Knowledge Extensions

**This campaign will underperform on engagement, permanently, and that is correct.**
Fear-based health content outperforms explanation by a wide margin on every platform
metric. Whoever runs this campaign should expect the comparison and should not respond
to it — the audience this reaches is the one that avoids the other kind, and reaching
them is the point.

**The awareness-day trap.** World Diabetes Day, Breast Cancer Awareness Month and their
equivalents are the standard vehicle for awareness content and they mostly produce
graphics that communicate nothing. An awareness day is a reason to publish, not a
subject. If the post would not be worth reading on an ordinary Tuesday, the day did not
make it worth reading. Occasion-driven content belongs to *Seasonal Campaigns*; this
campaign may use a date as timing, never as content.

**Clinical review is the same non-negotiable as in Medical Myths & Facts**, and for the
same reason. A named clinician must read every post, and the campaign should not run
before that person exists.

**What this file cannot supply.** The explanations themselves. Which subjects matter most
in this catchment, and what the accurate explanation is for each, require a clinician and
local knowledge.
