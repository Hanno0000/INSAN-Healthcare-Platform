# Purpose

This document is the Source of Truth for the **Hospital Life** campaign.

It describes a campaign, not a medical entity. Where a medical service file answers
*what is this department*, this file answers *what is this campaign, what belongs
inside it, and what must never appear in it.*

Five scheduled posts depend on it.

# Document Philosophy

Written for a worker that has never seen INSAN and cannot ask. Everything here is
derived from `MASTER_BRAND_ARCHITECTURE.md`, `AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md`,
`PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md`, `PROJECT_DECISIONS.md` and the patient-experience
material in `departments/MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md`.

This is the campaign most likely to become trivial. It is also the only one that can
change how it *feels* to walk into these buildings. Read **Human Insight** and
**Psychological Barriers** before writing anything.

# ═══════════ FOUNDATION ═══════════

## Overview

Hospital Life shows what it is actually like to be inside these hospitals — the
rhythm of a day, the texture of the place, the culture that has formed in it, and the
human moments that happen in a building where people spend the hardest days of their
lives.

Its subject is neither a person nor a process. Its subject is **the place and its
rhythm**: what the corridor is like at six in the morning, what Ramadan looks like on
a ward, what happens in a waiting room during a long operation.

## Definition

A supporting campaign that portrays the lived experience of the institution — its
daily rhythm, its atmosphere, its culture and its human moments — so that a stranger
can picture being there before they ever arrive.

**What is inside it:** the shape of a day and a night; the spaces patients and
families actually occupy; seasons and occasions as they land inside a hospital;
culture and small traditions; moments of ordinary humanity between people who did not
choose to meet; what the building sounds and looks like at hours nobody visits.

**What is outside it:**

- **A person as the subject.** If the post is about who someone is and what they
  decide, it is *Meet Our Team* — or *Meet Our Doctors* for a physician.
- **A process as the subject.** Sterilisation, handover, calibration, drills: that is
  *Behind The Scenes*.
- **A patient's story of their treatment.** That is *Patient Journey* or *Success
  Stories*.
- **Clinical or health education.** *Educational*.
- **Facility promotion.** A tour of a new wing is a hospital advertisement, not
  hospital life. The distinction is whether the content is about the space or about
  what happens in it.

### The routing test

Three supporting campaigns describe the same building. One question separates them:

| If the post is fundamentally about… | It belongs to |
|---|---|
| a person — who they are, what they decide | Meet Our Team |
| a process — what happens that nobody sees | Behind The Scenes |
| **a place and its rhythm — what it feels like here** | **Hospital Life** |

A post can contain all three. File it by what would be lost if you removed it: take
the *place and the moment* out of a Hospital Life post and there is nothing left. If
the same content would work filmed in any hospital anywhere, it is not this campaign
— it is stock sentiment.

## Vision

That a person who has never been to Future or Delta can already picture being there,
and that the picture is of somewhere calm, awake and humane — so that arriving feels
like recognition rather than exposure.

## Mission

Make the hardest building a person can enter into a place they can imagine
beforehand, and remember afterwards with dignity.

## Why This Service Exists

**A hospital does emotional work on everyone who enters it, whether or not anybody
manages that work.**

Nobody chooses to be in a hospital. People arrive frightened, at unusual hours, into a
building whose rules they do not know, and they are expected to behave normally while
their life is being decided in a room they cannot enter. The building is not neutral
in that experience: the length of the corridor, whether anyone made eye contact,
whether there was somewhere to sit, whether it was quiet at night — these become the
memory.

And the memory is durable in a way the medicine is not. Families forget the name of
the procedure. They do not forget the waiting room.

Two gaps follow, and this campaign closes both:

**Before arrival**, the unknown is a barrier. A patient postponing a consultation is
often not avoiding the diagnosis; they are avoiding the *ordeal* — the queue, the
confusion, the indignity they are expecting. Showing what it is actually like removes
a real reason people delay care.

**During and after**, the experience is either humane or it is not, and the
institution's culture is what decides. Publishing that culture is how it becomes
explicit, expected and, eventually, enforced. A hospital that publicly shows itself
being gentle at 3am has made a commitment to being gentle at 3am.

This is the campaign form of a decision already recorded.
`PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md` §3 names Human-Centered Care and Family Partnership as
the ecosystem's real competitive advantage — *the experience, not only the treatment*.
That advantage is invisible unless it is shown.

## Business Objectives

- Lower the emotional barrier to attending — the unknown is a real cause of delay.
- Differentiate on experience, which `PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md` §3 identifies as the
  ecosystem's genuine competitive ground.
- Support Delta's transformation narrative, where the visible change in atmosphere is
  the most credible evidence available.
- Strengthen recruitment and retention: people choose workplaces on culture, and this
  is culture published.
- Build the familiarity that makes a hospital the default choice before there is an
  emergency.

## Marketing Objectives

- Make the institution imaginable to someone who has never been.
- Replace the expected ordeal with a specific, calmer picture.
- Give the ecosystem's human-centred claim observable form.
- Occupy the seasons and occasions of Egyptian life inside a hospital setting, where
  they land differently and more truthfully than in ordinary seasonal advertising.
- Humanise without trivialising — the whole campaign lives or dies on that line.

## Positioning

For a patient or family who is dreading the hospital more than the illness, Hospital
Life shows what the days and nights are actually like inside — where competing
campaigns show facilities and equipment, this one shows the hours, the waiting and the
small human decisions that make up the experience, so the reader arrives already
knowing where they are.

> *Test applied:* a competitor can film a new wing and a smiling reception. Showing a
> night nurse lowering a light, or a family finishing iftar in a corridor, requires an
> institution willing to be seen at its ordinary moments rather than its best ones.

## Brand Philosophy

**Human before technology, and experience before service.**

`PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md` §5 states *Human Before Technology* — the human is the
hero and equipment is a tool. §3 states that the real advantage is the experience.
This campaign is what those two lines look like when they are executed rather than
declared.

`AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md` §7 requires the brand to be calm, warm, human and
honest, and forbids it being loud or over-promotional. Hospital Life is the campaign
where that personality is most directly on display, because it has the least to hide
behind.

## Core Promise

**Everything shown here is real: a real hour, in a real building, with the people
present giving their consent — no reconstruction, no performance, and nothing
prettier than the thing itself.**

The promise is about the honesty of the portrayal. It makes no claim about comfort
levels, waiting times or facilities, because those vary and cannot be promised.

# ═══════════ HUMAN UNDERSTANDING ═══════════

## Human Problem

What the patient or family is actually living through:

- *"I don't know what to expect. I don't know where to go or who to ask."*
- *"I'll be waiting for hours and nobody will tell me anything."*
- *"My mother is in there and I'm out here and I don't know what's happening."*
- *"Everyone's busy. I'm in the way."*
- *"It's the middle of the night and I don't know if I'm allowed to stay."*
- *"I've been in this corridor since morning. I haven't eaten. I can't leave."*
- *"I keep having to explain who I am to a different person."*
- *"I hate hospitals."* — the sentence underneath all the others.

Beneath these: **the family is going through the hardest hours of their life in a
building that does not acknowledge that this is what is happening to them.** They are
frightened, exhausted and expected to be patient, and the environment treats them as a
logistical fact.

For staff, a version of the same thing: they live a large part of their lives in this
building, at hours other people are asleep, in an emotional register no other workplace
demands — and it is invisible to everyone outside.

## Human Insight

**People do not remember hospitals by the medicine. They remember them by the small,
irrelevant details — and those details are doing all the emotional work, unsupervised.**

Ask anyone about a relative's hospital stay and they will not describe the treatment.
They will describe the chair they slept in, the tea someone brought at 2am, the nurse
who used their mother's name, the corridor with the broken light, the moment somebody
finally sat down to explain. Years later that is the entire memory, and it has
hardened into an opinion about the institution.

The uncomfortable half: **this is happening whether or not anyone is managing it.**
Every hospital is producing these memories continuously. Most are produced by
accident, by whoever happened to be on duty and how tired they were.

> Would a patient read it and think *"how did they know that?"* — yes. Everyone who
> has waited in a hospital knows their memory of it is made of irrelevant details, and
> no hospital has ever acknowledged that the irrelevant details were the experience.

The strategic consequence, which is the whole reason this campaign exists: **an
institution that publishes its small moments has decided to be responsible for them.**
Showing the tea at 2am is a commitment to the tea at 2am. This campaign is
simultaneously communication and an internal standard.

## Invisible Product

The visible product is a glimpse of life inside a hospital.

What the reader is really buying:

- **The end of the unknown.** A picture to replace the dread, which is usually worse
  than the reality.
- **Permission to be a person there.** Evidence that families are expected, not
  tolerated.
- **Advance familiarity.** Somewhere they have already, in a small way, been.
- **Dignity by anticipation.** The knowledge that they will not be processed.
- **Company.** Recognition that other people have sat in that chair and it was
  survivable.

The deepest one: **the belief that if the worst happens, it will happen somewhere
humane.** Nobody articulates this and everybody wants it.

## Psychological Transformation

**Before:**
> *"Going there will be an ordeal. I'll be lost, ignored and waiting, and I'll come
> out feeling like a number. I'll put it off."*

**After:**
> *"I know what it's like in there. It's calm, people talk to you, and there's
> somewhere to sit and someone to ask. I'd rather go now than wait."*

The movement is from **dread to familiarity** — and the business consequence is a
patient who attends earlier.

## Emotional Strategy

**Primary emotion: warmth** — the quiet kind produced by recognition, not the
manufactured kind produced by a soundtrack.

Supporting: calm, dignity, belonging, relief, gentle humour, respect.

Never: pity, sentimentality, triumph, staged joy, or grief used as material. And never
the register that treats a hospital as inspirational; illness is not a story arc, and
`AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md` §8 forbids emotional manipulation outright.

The single most useful instruction for this campaign's tone: **understate everything.**
The subject matter supplies all the emotion required. Any addition reads as
exploitation, and the audience detects it instantly because they have been in these
rooms.

## Audience

**Primary — patients and families who are avoiding, delaying or dreading a hospital.**
The largest and most underserved audience in healthcare communication. They are not
weighing hospitals; they are weighing whether to go at all.

**Primary — families currently attending.**
People in the building right now, who read the page while waiting. They are checking
whether their experience is what was promised, and they are the campaign's harshest
and most valuable critics.

**Secondary — current and prospective staff.**
Culture published is the most honest recruitment material an institution has. A nurse
deciding between employers is reading this campaign more carefully than any
advertisement.

**Secondary — the local community.**
Familiarity is what makes a hospital the default in an emergency, when nobody has time
to compare.

**Supporting — physicians and investors.**
Reading culture as an indicator of how the institution is run.

**Who decides, who influences, who pays:** the patient or an adult child decides; the
family member who last spent a night there influences most; the patient or an insurer
pays.

## Audience Decision

**What they compare:** their memory of the last hospital they were in; what a relative
reported; how it looked on the way past; distance.

**What they fear:** waiting without information; indignity; being separated from their
relative; being a nuisance; the night.

**Where they look:** relatives first, then social media — and this campaign is one of
the few that performs on the platform where they are actually looking.

**What actually moves them:** a specific hour. *"Visiting ends at eight, but nobody is
asked to leave a parent who is frightened"* moves a reader more than any statement
about patient-centred care, because it is a rule about a real moment they can picture
themselves in.

# ═══════════ MEDICAL FOUNDATION ═══════════

## Service Boundaries

- This campaign does **not** show identifiable patients without documented consent,
  and never in distress, undressed, sedated or unable to consent.
- It does **not** show clinical procedures.
- It does **not** show or imply a patient's diagnosis, condition or data.
- It does **not** use grief, death or serious deterioration as content. Ever.
- It does **not** promise comfort, waiting times, visiting rules or facilities.
- It does **not** portray a hospital as a pleasant place to be. It portrays it as a
  humane place to be when you have to be.
- It does **not** feature staff as its subject — that is *Meet Our Team*.
- It does **not** tour facilities as an advertisement for the building.

## Medical Philosophy

The experience is part of the care, not a service wrapped around it.
`MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md` states this directly about families: *the family experience is
not secondary to clinical care; it is part of the care system*, and families who feel
informed and included cope better.

This campaign follows from that. The environment, the rhythm and the culture are
clinical factors, not decoration — which is why portraying them is a legitimate
communication of quality and not a soft alternative to it.

## Core Principles

**Show the hour, not the highlight.** Ordinary time is the subject. A hospital at its
most ordinary is more persuasive than a hospital at its best.

**Understate.** The material is already emotional. Add nothing.

**Consent, always, and revocable.** Anyone recognisable — patient, family, staff — has
agreed specifically, and can withdraw. In a hospital, a person's presence in the
building is itself private information.

**Never use suffering.** Difficulty may be present; it is never the point, and it is
never the hook.

**Specific to these buildings.** If the post could have been filmed in any hospital in
the world, it is not this campaign. The particular corridor is what makes it true.

**No promises about the experience.** Show what happened, not what always happens.
A visiting rule shown once becomes an expectation for every family.

## Core Features

The material available to this campaign, by category. These are the shapes a Hospital
Life post can take; the specific instances must be observed rather than invented.

**The rhythm of a day**
- Dawn: the shift change, the first light in a ward, the building waking.
- Morning: rounds, outpatient clinics filling, the busiest and most legible hours.
- Afternoon: the quiet middle, families arriving, the long waits.
- Evening: visiting hours, the emotional peak of the day.
- Night: the hours nobody sees. The most distinctive material this campaign has.

**The spaces families actually occupy**
- The waiting area outside a critical unit — where the hardest hours are spent.
- Reception, and the first ninety seconds of a hospital visit.
- Corridors, benches, stairwells, the informal places people end up.
- The prayer space.
- The cafeteria at unusual hours.
- The area outside theatre, during an operation.

**Seasons and occasions, as they land inside a hospital**
- Ramadan: iftar on a ward, the timing of medication, staff breaking fast between
  cases, families bringing food. The single richest seasonal territory available.
- Eid: the ward decorated, the patients who cannot go home, visits.
- Mother's Day, exam season, the first cold week, the summer heat and what it does to
  an emergency department.
- National and awareness days, where they genuinely connect.

**Culture and small traditions**
- How staff greet patients and each other.
- What happens on someone's last day, or first.
- The informal customs a ward has developed.
- Long-standing relationships — the patient who has come back for years.

**Human moments between strangers**
- Two families in a waiting room who did not know each other in the morning.
- Someone giving up a seat, sharing food, holding a place in a queue.
- A child in an outpatient clinic being distracted while blood is taken.
- An older patient being walked to the door rather than pointed at it.

**The building itself**
- Light through a window at a particular hour.
- The sound of a ward at night, and how quiet it is kept.
- The view from a window a patient spends a week looking at.
- Seasonal and weather changes seen from inside.

## Differentiators

**It shows ordinary hours.** Every competitor shows facilities and events. Almost none
show a Tuesday afternoon, and the ordinary hour is what a patient is actually trying to
imagine.

**It occupies the night.** Nobody photographs a hospital at 3am, and it is the hour the
audience is most afraid of. This is the campaign's most distinctive available
territory.

**Seasonal content that is true rather than decorative.** Ramadan in a hospital is
genuinely different from Ramadan in an advertisement. This campaign can occupy the
highest-engagement windows of the Egyptian year with material nobody else has.

**It is evidence for the experience claim.** `PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md` §3 puts the
experience at the centre of the ecosystem's competitive advantage. This is the only
campaign that shows it directly.

**It is the same ecosystem in two buildings.** A shared culture visible at both Future
and Delta demonstrates the platform in the register — atmosphere — where a platform is
least expected to be visible and therefore most convincing.

**It is where Delta's transformation is legible.** A changed institution feels
different before it can prove anything, and feeling is what this campaign captures.

## Service Journey

The experience as a family actually lives it — and therefore the sequence available to
this campaign:

1. **The decision to come.** Dread, delay, the argument with a relative.
2. **Arrival.** The car park, the door, the first person met.
3. **The first wait.** Before anyone has explained anything.
4. **Being taken through.** The moment of separation from the family.
5. **The long middle.** Hours in a corridor with no information.
6. **The update.** When somebody sits down and says something true.
7. **The night.** Whether to stay, whether they are allowed to, where they sleep.
8. **Going home, or not.** Discharge, or another night.
9. **Coming back.** Follow-up, and the difference familiarity makes.

Steps 3, 5 and 7 are where the experience is decided and where almost no institution
looks. They are this campaign's centre.

## What Makes This Different

For a worker reading nothing else: **choose one real hour in one real place, show what
ordinarily happens in it, and add nothing.** No hero, no lesson, no claim.

# ═══════════ MARKETING INTELLIGENCE ═══════════

## Communication Objective

To replace dread with a picture.

The reader is carrying an imagined hospital that is worse than the real one — longer,
colder, more indifferent. The post's job is to give them a specific, ordinary,
truthful image to put in its place, so that attending becomes thinkable.

## What We Are Really Selling

**Dignity.**

The visible content is life inside a hospital. What is being sold is the promise
underneath it: that in the worst hours of your life, you will be treated as a person
and not as a case — and that this is a property of the institution, not of whoever
happens to be on shift.

Restated for a worker: we are not selling comfort, facilities or friendliness. We are
selling **the certainty of being treated humanely**, which is the one thing every
patient wants and almost none will ask for.

## Human Truth

**People remember how they were treated on their worst days for the rest of their
lives.**

Recorded almost word for word in `MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md`: *people remember how they
were treated during their most difficult days far longer than they remember the medical
equipment they saw.*

A second truth this campaign rests on: **familiarity reduces fear more reliably than
reassurance does.** Telling someone not to worry does very little. Showing them the
room does a great deal.

## Audience Psychology

How the audience thinks before anything is said:

- **Hospitals are places of indignity.** Waiting, exposure, powerlessness, explaining
  yourself repeatedly.
- **The staff are busy and you are an interruption.** Which stops people asking the
  questions that would help them most.
- **Nights are frightening and unstaffed.** Formed from experience elsewhere.
- **Everything shown publicly is staged.** So warmth in an advertisement reads as
  evidence of the opposite.
- **Being ill is shameful.** Rarely stated, deeply felt, and a real driver of delay —
  which is why portraying patients with dignity has clinical value.

## Emotional Triggers

**Primary:** warmth.

**Supporting:** calm, dignity, belonging, relief, recognition, gentle humour.

Deliberately excluded: pity, inspiration, triumph, grief, urgency and nostalgia.
Illness is not a journey and a hospital is not a stage. `AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md`
§8 forbids the register outright, and here it would also destroy the campaign's only
asset, which is being believed.

## Psychological Barriers

**Barrier 1 — "This is staged."**
The strongest and most justified scepticism this campaign faces, and the reason it
must be visually ordinary. A perfectly lit, perfectly composed hospital moment is
self-defeating. Corrected by choosing moments no one would stage: an awkward hour, an
unremarkable corridor, a real queue.

**Barrier 2 — "They're making a nice story out of sick people."**
The accusation the campaign is one bad post away from at all times. Corrected by never
making the patient the material: the subject is the hour and the place, and the person
is present only with consent and only with dignity.

**Barrier 3 — "It's not like that when I go."**
The most damaging failure, because it converts the campaign into evidence of dishonesty
and it is verifiable by anyone. Corrected by showing only what is ordinary and
repeatable, never the exceptional day, and never as a promise.

**Barrier 4 — "I hate hospitals."**
A blanket avoidance that no argument reaches. Corrected only by familiarity, slowly,
across many posts. This is why the campaign is a sustained one and cannot be judged
post by post.

**Barrier 5 — "Nice atmosphere, but is the medicine any good?"**
Warmth without competence reads as compensation. Corrected by keeping this campaign
adjacent to *Behind The Scenes* and *Meet Our Team* in the plan — the atmosphere is
credible when the audience has also seen the rigour.

## Core Messaging Framework

Consistently reinforced:

- You will be treated as a person here, including at 3am.
- Families are expected, not tolerated.
- The hard hours are recognised, not ignored.
- The place is calm because it is organised.
- The same culture exists in both hospitals, because INSAN sets the standard.
- Nothing here is performed for you; this is what the day is like.

## Messaging Hierarchy

1. **The moment** — one hour, one place, one thing happening.
2. **The human detail inside it** — the small specific that makes it true.
3. **What it says about how this place works** — implied, never stated as a claim.
4. **The standard** — that this is ordinary here, in both hospitals.
5. **INSAN** — only where it arises naturally. Often it should not appear at all.

Never reverse it. A post that opens with the standard has written a brand statement
with a photograph attached, and this campaign dies on brand statements.

If there is only one line, it is the first: the moment.

## Narrative Themes

- **The night.** Six hours nobody sees, told quietly.
- **The waiting room.** The hardest room in the building and the least documented.
- **Ramadan inside a hospital.** Iftar on a ward, medication timing, staff breaking
  fast between cases.
- **Eid for the people who cannot go home.**
- **The shift change.** The building handing itself over.
- **First light.** A ward at dawn.
- **The long operation.** The corridor outside, and the hours in it.
- **Strangers.** Two families who met that morning.
- **The regular.** A patient who has come back for years, and what that relationship
  looks like.
- **Going home.** The last hour, the paperwork, the door.
- **The child in the clinic.** How fear is handled when the patient is six.
- **The building in weather.** Heat, rain, the first cold week, seen from inside.
- **The same hour in two hospitals.** Future and Delta at 3am, side by side.
- **The room with the window.** What a patient looks at for a week.

## Content Pillars

**1. The Hours** — dawn, the long afternoon, the evening, the night.
**2. The Waiting** — the spaces and hours families occupy, taken seriously.
**3. Seasons and Occasions** — Ramadan, Eid, national and awareness days, weather.
**4. Small Humanity** — moments between people who did not choose to meet.
**5. The Culture** — how this place behaves when nothing is happening.
**6. The Place Itself** — light, sound, quiet, the building as a lived environment.

## Storytelling Opportunities

Moments, not topics — each specific, filmable, and ordinary:

- The corridor at 5:40am, before the first clinic.
- A night nurse lowering a light rather than switching it off.
- Iftar being eaten in a staff room between two cases, quickly.
- A family sharing dates with strangers in a waiting area at sunset.
- The chair a relative has slept in, at 6am, with a blanket that was found for them.
- Reception writing directions on a slip of paper instead of pointing.
- A child being given something to hold before a blood test.
- The bench outside theatre during a long operation, and the clock above it.
- Two nurses handing over at 7am, one arriving and one leaving.
- An older patient being walked to the door and out to the car.
- The prayer space, empty, in the middle of the afternoon.
- Rain on a window in a room where someone has been for a week.
- A ward decorated for Eid by staff, badly and sincerely.
- The last patient leaving an outpatient clinic, and the lights going off behind them.
- The same wall clock at Future and at Delta, both showing 3am.
- Somebody sitting down to explain something, rather than standing.

## Communication Style

Always: quiet, specific, warm, unhurried, observational, honest.

Never: sentimental, inspirational, dramatic, promotional, or written as though the
hospital is proud of itself.

The voice is closest to good documentary photography with a caption — the image
carries the meaning and the words say only what the image cannot.

## Tone of Voice

Write as someone who was present and noticed something, not as an institution
presenting itself.

Short sentences. Ordinary words. No adjectives doing emotional work — if a sentence
needs "heartwarming" or "touching" to land, the moment was not strong enough and
should be replaced rather than decorated.

The most reliable test for this campaign: **read it aloud to someone who has recently
sat in a hospital waiting room.** If any line would make them feel their experience had
been used, rewrite it.

## Messaging Rules

- Always anchor to a specific hour and a specific place.
- Always keep the moment ordinary and repeatable.
- Always obtain and record consent from anyone recognisable.
- Always let the image carry the emotion; the words stay flat.
- Never show a patient in distress, undressed, sedated, or unable to consent.
- Never use grief, death or deterioration as content.
- Never promise a facility, a visiting rule, a waiting time or a comfort.
- Never stage a moment, and never present a reconstruction as observation.
- Never make the hospital look pleasant. Make it look humane.
- Never use the inspirational register, and never call anything a journey.
- Never let a post work equally well filmed in any other hospital.
- Never attach a booking ask to a moment of someone's hardest day.

# ═══════════ OFFERS ═══════════

## Current Offers

**No offers.** This campaign advertises nothing. An offer attached to a Hospital Life
post converts a real human moment into a sales device, which is the precise failure
`AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md` §21 exists to prevent and the one the audience punishes
most severely.

## Offer Rules

- No promotional offer, price, discount or package may appear in this campaign.
- No facility or comfort may be presented as a premium tier. Dignity is not an
  upgrade.
- Seasonal posts must never carry a seasonal offer. Ramadan content that ends in a
  promotion is the single worst available use of this campaign.

## Call To Action

In priority order, and all of them soft:

1. **None.** Most posts should ask for nothing at all. This is the campaign where an
   ask is most likely to damage the post.
2. **Come and see** — an open, unpressured invitation, appropriate only where the post
   is about a space or an outpatient service.
3. **Follow the page**, for people building familiarity over time.
4. **Ask us** — where a post touches a rule families genuinely need to know, such as
   staying overnight with a relative.

Never a booking CTA on a post that features a patient, a family or a hard hour.

## Primary KPI

**Comments and saves — specifically comments describing the reader's own experience.**

Chosen deliberately over reach. This campaign succeeds when it makes people talk about
their own hospital memories, because that is the moment dread is replaced by
recognition. A post with high reach and no comments has been scrolled past; a post with
forty people telling their own stories has changed something.

Secondary: staff sentiment. If staff share it, it was true.

# ═══════════ STRATEGIC GOVERNANCE ═══════════

## Strategic Reminder

Before publishing, ask what the reader believes afterwards.

If the answer is *"what a lovely hospital"*, the post failed — it advertised a
building. If it is *"if I had to be there, I'd be alright"*, it worked.

And ask the second question, which belongs to this campaign alone: **would the person
in this post be glad it was published?** If there is any doubt, it does not run.

## Strategic Constraints

- Never use a patient's or family's difficulty as material.
- Never compete by implying other hospitals are cold or chaotic.
- Never publish an exceptional day as though it were an ordinary one.
- Never let production value exceed the truth of the moment — a beautifully shot
  hospital reads as a set.
- Consent outranks every campaign consideration and is revocable without discussion.
- Long-term brand value outranks short-term engagement
  (`AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md` §20.5). This campaign has the highest short-term
  engagement potential of any supporting campaign and therefore the greatest
  temptation to breach that rule.

## Medical Communication Principles

- Nothing here constitutes medical advice.
- No patient's condition, diagnosis or prognosis may be visible, stated or inferable.
- Presence in a hospital is itself confidential. A recognisable patient requires
  consent even when nothing clinical is shown.
- Where a moment involves a child, consent comes from the guardian and the bar is
  higher, not equal.
- Nothing shown may imply a standard of comfort or attention the institution cannot
  sustain.

## Never Promise

The campaign must never promise:

- Waiting times, visiting hours, or the right to stay overnight.
- Any facility, amenity, private room or level of comfort.
- That any specific person will be present or available.
- That every shift, ward or day looks like the one shown.
- Any clinical outcome, or any connection between atmosphere and outcome.
- That the experience is better than another institution's.
- Emotional support beyond what the ecosystem actually provides.

## Can Promise

The campaign can confidently state:

- That families are treated as partners in care
  (`PLATFORM_KNOWLEDGE_BASE.md` §3, `MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md`).
- That human-centred care — respect for the patient's time, privacy and understanding
  — is a stated operating value of the ecosystem.
- That the same standard of experience is intended at Future and at Delta, because
  INSAN sets it (`MASTER_BRAND_ARCHITECTURE.md` §3).
- That what was shown genuinely happened, at that hour, in that place.
- That everyone recognisable consented.

## Brand Perception

After sustained exposure, the audience should believe:

**"If I ever have to go there, I'll be alright. They notice people."**

Staff should believe: *"That's actually what it's like. They didn't make it into an
advertisement."*

## Long-Term Brand Contribution

This campaign builds familiarity, and familiarity is what decides where an ambulance
goes and where a frightened family drives at 2am — the decisions made with no time to
compare.

It is also the ecosystem's most durable emotional asset. Facilities date and equipment
is replaced; the memory of having been treated like a person does not. Over years, this
campaign accumulates into the thing a hospital cannot buy: a community that already
feels it knows the place.

For Delta specifically, it is the most credible transformation evidence available. A
changed institution feels different long before it can prove anything, and this is the
only campaign that captures feeling.

## Relationship With INSAN

**Trust platform: Continuity.**

INSAN's promise is that a patient meets the same system and the same standard wherever
they go inside the ecosystem. Atmosphere is where that promise is hardest to fake and
easiest to feel — two buildings with the same culture is an argument no single hospital
can make, and no competitor can assemble quickly.

The strongest format available to this campaign follows from that: the same hour,
filmed at Future and at Delta, side by side. It says *one standard, two places* without
a word of corporate language.

Cross-platform reinforcement per `AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md` §16 must be especially
light here. INSAN often should not appear at all: a branded frame around a family's 3am
is exactly the intrusion this campaign is built to avoid. Let the ecosystem be visible
across the body of work rather than inside each post.

## Institutional Knowledge Extensions

**This is the campaign most likely to be executed badly, and the failure mode is
specific: it becomes birthday cakes.** Staff parties, celebrations, decorated
corridors and cheerful group photographs are the easiest content to produce and they
communicate nothing — worse, they read as an institution enjoying itself while
somebody's father is in theatre. The discipline is to point the camera at the ordinary
hard hours instead, which is harder, slower and the entire value of the campaign.

**Ramadan is the highest-value window in the Egyptian calendar and the system has no
way to plan for it.** Audit B recorded that nothing in Campaign OS knows a season is
coming (B10), and the proposed events calendar (I5) is not built. Until it is, Ramadan
and Eid content for this campaign must be planned manually and early — the material
only exists if someone was in the building filming at iftar, which cannot be arranged
retrospectively.

**Consent in this campaign is different from consent elsewhere.** In *Meet Our Team*,
consent is a professional agreement with a colleague. Here it is frequently asked of a
frightened family in a corridor, and the power imbalance is severe: a relative who
believes their patient's care might be affected cannot meaningfully refuse. The rule
that follows is absolute — **never ask a family for consent while they are waiting on
news.** Ask afterwards, or use the moment without them in it.

**The night is the campaign's most valuable territory and its most difficult.** It is
what the audience fears, it is what nobody else shows, and it is the hardest to film —
low light, fewer staff, more vulnerable patients, and the least capacity to manage a
crew. Plan for it deliberately rather than hoping it will happen.

**What this file cannot supply.** It does not know the actual layout of either
hospital, its visiting rules, the shape of its day, its local customs or what the
waiting areas are like. Every specific must be observed. A Hospital Life post written
from imagination is not merely weak — it will be recognised as false by every reader
who has been in the building, which is the audience the campaign most needs.

**Apply the routing test before writing.** If the post's subject is a person or a
