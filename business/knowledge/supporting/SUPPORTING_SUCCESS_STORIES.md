# ═══════════ FOUNDATION ═══════════

## Overview

Success Stories tells what happened to real patients inside the ecosystem, in their
own words, with their documented consent.

It is the most persuasive campaign available and the most dangerous. A patient
account is the only content type the audience treats as evidence rather than
advertising — and that credibility is exactly what makes an inaccurate, coerced or
misleading story so damaging.

## Definition

A supporting campaign presenting real patient and family experiences of care inside
the ecosystem, with documented consent, focused on the experience rather than the
clinical result.

**What is inside it:** a real person's account of what the experience was like; what
they were afraid of; what they found; how they were treated; what the care meant to
them and their family. The human experience of being a patient here.

**What is outside it:** medical outcome claims; before-and-after clinical
comparisons; anything presented as typical or predictive; any story without
documented consent; any story where consent could not be freely given; stories used
to imply that a similar patient will have a similar result.

**The distinction from Patient Journey:** Patient Journey is the path, generalised
and anonymous. Success Stories is a person, specific and consented. If it names
someone, it is this campaign — and every rule below applies.

## Vision

That someone facing the same fear reads a real account and thinks *"that could be
me"* — about the treatment they received as a person, never about the medical result
they can expect.

## Mission

Tell true stories, told by the people who lived them, with consent that was freely
given and can be withdrawn — and never let a story imply a promise.

## Why This Service Exists

Every claim a hospital makes about itself is discounted by the audience, correctly.
Institutions describing their own quality are the least credible source available on
the subject.

A patient describing their own experience is a different kind of statement. It is
not a claim about the institution; it is a report from someone with nothing to sell.
That is why it persuades, and why it is the only content type that can carry the
weight of *"this is what it is actually like."*

The campaign exists because the ecosystem's core proposition — organisation,
communication, dignity, continuity — is experiential. It cannot be demonstrated by
describing it. Somebody has to have lived it and be willing to say so.

It also exists to serve a purpose beyond persuasion: a person facing the same
diagnosis, at 2am, searching for what this is going to be like. For that reader, an
honest account is genuinely useful, and the campaign should be judged partly on
whether it helps them.

## Business Objectives

- Build credible evidence for the ecosystem's experience claims.
- Support Delta's trust-rebuilding through demonstrated experience rather than
  assertion — integrated into general content, per `PROJECT_DECISIONS.md` §4.
- Strengthen confidence among patients hesitating over a decision.
- Provide referring physicians with evidence of how their patients are treated.
- Support investor and contract confidence with experience evidence.

## Marketing Objectives

- Convert the ecosystem's experiential claims into first-person evidence.
- Reduce the fear of the unknown for patients facing a similar situation.
- Show, rather than assert, the values every other campaign describes.
- Build a library of consented material reusable across the website, referral
  communication and recruitment.

## Positioning

For a patient facing something frightening who has stopped believing what
institutions say about themselves, Success Stories offers first-person accounts of
what the experience was actually like — where competing testimonial campaigns
showcase recoveries and imply results, this one is explicitly about how a person was
treated, and refuses to make the outcome the point.

> *Test applied:* any hospital can publish a grateful patient. Publishing accounts
> that deliberately exclude the medical result, and saying why, is a position a
> results-led testimonial campaign cannot occupy without abandoning itself.

## Brand Philosophy

**The story is about the treatment of a person, never about the outcome of a case.**

This single rule separates a legitimate experience campaign from a false-hope
machine. Medicine does not guarantee results; a campaign built on good results
implies that it does, and it does so most powerfully to the readers who are most
desperate.

`AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md` §21 prohibits unproven claims and emotional
manipulation. A recovery story presented as representative is both.

## Core Promise

**Every story published is true, told by the person who lived it, with their
documented and freely given consent — and is never presented as a predictor of
anyone else's result.**

The promise is about the integrity of the telling. It says nothing about outcomes,
because it must not.

# ═══════════ HUMAN UNDERSTANDING ═══════════

## Human Problem

What the reader is living through:

- *"I don't know what this is going to be like."*
- *"Everyone tells me it'll be fine. Nobody tells me what actually happens."*
- *"I don't know anyone who's been through this."*
- *"Am I overreacting, or not reacting enough?"*
- *"I need to hear from someone who isn't being paid to reassure me."*
- *"I'm frightened and I can't say that to my family, because they're frightened
  too."*

And for the person considering telling their story:

- *"Do I want my medical history public?"*
- *"Will my employer see this? My neighbours?"*
- *"They looked after me — I feel I owe them this."*

That last one is the most important line in this document. **Gratitude is a form of
pressure.** A patient who feels indebted cannot give consent as freely as one who
does not, and the campaign has to be built with that asymmetry in mind.

## Human Insight

**People facing something frightening are not looking for reassurance. They are
looking for accuracy — and a story that is too good is read as advertising and
discarded.**

The instinct is to publish the best outcome and the most grateful patient. That
story reaches the audience already inclined to believe, and bounces off the one who
is genuinely afraid — because a frightened reader is a suspicious reader. Perfection
reads as selection.

The story that lands is the one containing something the institution would rather
not include: the wait that was long, the night that was hard, the moment the patient
was angry. **Credibility comes from the parts that are not flattering**, and a
campaign that removes them removes the reason anyone believes it.

> Would a patient read this and think *"how did they know that?"* — yes. Everyone who
> has searched for accounts of a diagnosis has skipped the glossy ones and looked for
> the person describing what it was really like.

## Invisible Product

The visible product is a patient's story.

What the reader is really buying:

- **Not being the first.** Someone has walked this and can describe it.
- **Accurate expectation** — what it is actually like, including the hard parts.
- **Company** in a fear they cannot voice at home.
- **Evidence they can weigh themselves**, rather than a claim to accept.
- **Permission to be frightened** and to go anyway.

Never hope of a particular outcome. If a reader finishes a story expecting the same
result, the story was told wrongly.

## Psychological Transformation

**Before:**
> *"I don't know what I'm walking into, and everything I read is either terrifying or
> selling me something."*

**After:**
> *"I know what it's like now. Someone real went through it and told me honestly —
> including the hard parts. I'm still frightened, but I'm not walking in blind."*

The movement is from **isolated dread** to **informed apprehension**.

Note the endpoint. It is not confidence and it is not hope of recovery. Fear is
appropriate; the campaign removes the ignorance around it, not the fear itself.

## Emotional Strategy

**Primary emotion: recognition** — *that is someone like me, and this is what it was
actually like.*

Supporting: relief, dignity, gratitude, human connection, honesty.

Never: hope of a specific outcome, triumph, pity, or the survivor-as-inspiration
framing that turns a patient into a device.

**The manipulation risk here is higher than anywhere else in the portfolio.** A
sincere, emotional patient account is inherently powerful and can be pushed a few
degrees into exploitation without anyone intending it. The test is whether the
emotion serves the reader's understanding or the brand's persuasion.

## Audience

**Primary — patients facing a similar situation.**
Recently diagnosed, awaiting a procedure, deciding whether to seek care. Frightened,
searching, and sceptical of institutional voices.

**Secondary — the family of such a patient.**
Often searching on the patient's behalf, sometimes without telling them.

**Supporting — the general audience**, forming an impression of the ecosystem over
time.

**Also — referring physicians**, reading how their patients are treated.

**And — the contributor themselves and their family.** They will see it published
and so will everyone who knows them. A campaign that embarrasses a contributor
loses the next one.

**Who decides, who influences, who pays:** the patient decides; the family
influences heavily and often searches first; an insurer, employer or family pays.

## Audience Decision

**What they compare:** whether anyone like them has been through it here; whether
the account sounds real; what a relative said; distance and cost.

**What they fear:** being a case rather than a person; being left uninformed;
something going wrong and nobody telling them; and — specific to this campaign —
being manipulated by a story that was selected or shaped.

**Where they look:** search, social media, and people they know. Increasingly they
look for accounts from patients rather than statements from institutions, which is
exactly why this campaign exists and why its honesty is load-bearing.

**What moves them:** specificity and imperfection. A named fear, an ordinary detail,
a hard night described honestly. Polished gratitude moves nobody who is genuinely
afraid.

# ═══════════ MEDICAL FOUNDATION ═══════════

## Service Boundaries

- This campaign does **not** publish clinical outcomes as achievements.
- It does **not** present any story as typical, expected or predictive.
- It does **not** publish without documented, freely given, withdrawable consent.
- It does **not** publish a story from a patient still in active care, where consent
  cannot be free of the treatment relationship.
- It does **not** publish stories involving children, decedents, or patients unable
  to consent for themselves, except under the additional conditions below.
- It does **not** give clinical advice or describe protocols.
- It does **not** use a story to promote a specific service, physician or offer.

## Medical Philosophy

Medicine cannot promise results, and communication must not do by implication what
medicine will not do directly.

`MEDICAL_SERVICE_ICU.md` states the principle for the whole ecosystem: the promise
is about the system, the organisation and the experience — never about clinical
outcomes. A recovery story published as evidence of capability breaks that promise
while appearing to keep it, because nobody has technically claimed anything.

**Therefore: the medical result is context, never the point.** A story may say what
happened. It may never present what happened as what the institution achieves, or as
what a reader can expect.

## Core Principles

**Consent is documented, specific, informed and withdrawable.** Not verbal. Not
general. The contributor knows exactly what will be published, where, and for how
long — and can withdraw at any time, after which the material comes down.

**Consent must be free of the care relationship.** Not sought during an admission,
not by the treating clinician, and not while the patient is dependent on the person
asking. Gratitude and dependence both compromise consent, and neither is visible in
a signed form.

**Their words, unedited in substance.** Tidy grammar if needed. Never improve the
sentiment, sharpen the message or add a line they did not say.

**Include what is not flattering.** The long wait, the frightening night, the thing
that went less well. This is a rule, not a permission: a story with no friction is
not credible and should not be published.

**Outcome is never the achievement.** Say what happened if the contributor wants it
said. Never frame it as a result the institution produces.

**No implication of typicality.** No "many of our patients", no "results like these",
no framing that invites the reader to expect the same.

**Protect the contributor from their own generosity.** People in gratitude
over-disclose. Publishing everything a patient offers is not consent, it is
extraction — the campaign has an obligation to hold back material the contributor
may regret.

**Dignity over persuasion, always.** If the most persuasive version is the less
dignified one, publish the dignified one.

## Core Features

What this campaign contains.

**Story types**

- **Experience accounts** — what the care was like, in the patient's words. The
  backbone of the campaign.
- **Family accounts** — from the relative who was there, particularly for Intensive
  Care and Emergency, where the family is the primary audience.
- **A moment, not a case** — one interaction that mattered. Often the strongest
  format, and the lowest risk.
- **Returning patients** — why someone came back.
- **The difficult story**, where the contributor wants it told and it can be told
  without implying failure or blame. Handled with unusual care and additional
  review.

**What every story must contain**

- Documented consent reference.
- The contributor's own words.
- What they were afraid of before.
- Something specific about how they were treated.
- At least one unflattering or difficult element.
- No outcome framed as an achievement.

**What is recorded but never published**

- Medical record details beyond what the contributor chose to share.
- Diagnosis specifics, unless the contributor explicitly consented to them.
- Anything identifying another patient.
- Staff names, unless separately consented by that staff member.

**Formats:** quote card · short written account · carousel · video interview ·
family account · a moment-in-time post. Video carries the highest consent burden and
the highest withdrawal cost — a video cannot be meaningfully un-seen once shared.

**Cadence:** infrequent by design. A steady stream of testimonials reads as
manufactured; six scheduled posts across the plan is appropriate.

## Differentiators

What does not differentiate:

- Publishing testimonials. Everyone does.
- Grateful patients.
- Before-and-after framing — which this campaign prohibits.
- Emotional music over a recovery montage.

What does:

- **The outcome is deliberately not the point**, and the campaign says so.
- **Unflattering detail is required**, not tolerated.
- **Consent standards stated publicly** — a campaign that describes how it obtains
  consent is making a claim about its ethics that can be checked.
- **The family's account treated as equal**, not as a supplement to the patient's.
- **Ordinary moments over dramatic ones.** A nurse who explained something at 3am
  carries more weight than a rescue.
- **Restraint.** Publishing rarely, and refusing stories that would work, is itself
  the differentiator — and it is the one competitors find hardest to copy, because
  it costs them reach.

## Service Journey

How a Success Story is produced. **No step may be skipped.**

1. **Identification** — a patient or family who has expressed willingness
   unprompted, or who is approached well after care has concluded.
2. **Approach** — by someone outside the treating relationship. Never the treating
   clinician. The invitation makes clear that declining changes nothing.
3. **Informed consent** — documented, specific: what is published, where, in what
   formats, for how long, and how to withdraw. Given without time pressure.
4. **The conversation** — a real interview. Open questions. What they were afraid
   of, what surprised them, what was hard.
5. **Drafting** — their words. Friction retained. Outcome de-emphasised.
6. **Clinical review** — no protocol detail, no implied outcome claim, nothing
   medically misleading.
7. **Contributor review and approval** — they read the final version and approve it
   before publication. Approval, not notification.
8. **Publication** — with the consent reference recorded against the asset.
9. **Withdrawal readiness** — a documented route to removal, honoured promptly and
   without argument.

## What Makes This Different

For a worker reading nothing else:

Real people, their real words, documented consent, and **the outcome is never the
point**. Include what was hard. Never imply that another patient will experience the
same. If in doubt, do not publish — the story that gets away costs less than the one
that should not have run.

# ═══════════ MARKETING INTELLIGENCE ═══════════

## Communication Objective

Not *"prove that we get good results"* — that is the campaign this one exists in
opposition to.

The objective is to let a frightened person hear, from someone with nothing to sell,
what being a patient here is actually like.

## What We Are Really Selling

**Accurate expectation, from a credible source.**

Never hope of a result. Never the institution's capability. The product is a true
account of an experience, offered to someone who needs to know what they are walking
into.

## Human Truth

**People believe other people, and disbelieve institutions — and they are right to.**

The corollary is the discipline this campaign requires: the moment a patient's story
starts sounding like the institution wrote it, the credibility that made it valuable
is gone, and it cannot be recovered by making the next one more sincere.

## Audience Psychology

Before anything is said, the audience believes:

- Testimonials are selected, and the bad ones are not published.
- Patients who appear in hospital advertising were probably compensated.
- Every story shown is a best case.
- If it sounds too good, it was written by the marketing department.
- Real accounts are found on forums, not on hospital pages.

**Every one of these beliefs is reasonable**, and most testimonial campaigns confirm
them. The strategy is not to argue against the scepticism but to satisfy it: publish
the friction, state the consent standard, and let the reader notice that this does
not behave like the campaigns they distrust.

## Emotional Triggers

Primary: **recognition.**

Supporting: relief, honesty, dignity, human connection, gratitude, calm.

Never: hope of a specific outcome, triumph, pity, inspiration-at-the-patient's-
expense, fear of not acting.

## Psychological Barriers

**"These are cherry-picked."**
The central barrier. Defeated only by including difficulty — a story containing
something the hospital would rather omit is the one piece of evidence that the
selection is not total.

**"They were paid."**
Defeated by never compensating contributors, and by being able to say so.

**"That won't be me."**
Correct, and the campaign must agree with it rather than argue. Stories are about
treatment, not results, precisely so the reader is never invited to expect the same
outcome.

**"I don't want my medical history public."**
A barrier to *contributing*, and the campaign's supply depends on it. Defeated by
visible consent standards, control over what is published, and a real withdrawal
route.

**"This is exploiting sick people."**
Defeated by conduct: no compensation, consent outside the care relationship,
contributor approval, restraint in what is published.

## Core Messaging Framework

- These are real people, in their own words, with their consent.
- The story is about how someone was treated, not about a medical result.
- Nothing here predicts anyone else's experience.
- The difficult parts are included.
- Contributors choose what is shared and may withdraw.
- What is consistent across stories is the standard, not the outcome.
- INSAN develops the system these experiences happen inside.

## Messaging Hierarchy

The person → what they feared → what the experience was actually like → what they
were left with → the standard that shaped it → INSAN → an invitation to learn more.

**Never open with the institution.** And never close on the outcome.

## Narrative Themes

- **What I was most afraid of** — the fear named, before anything else.
- **The moment someone explained.**
- **The night that was hard**, told honestly.
- **Someone who remembered my name.**
- **What I wish I'd known** — the most useful post the campaign can produce.
- **My mother's account** — the family's experience, standing on its own.
- **Coming back**, and why.
- **The thing that surprised me** — usually small and never clinical.
- **What I'd tell someone about to go through this.**
- **The person who wasn't a doctor** — the porter, the receptionist, the cleaner.

## Content Pillars

1. **The Fear Before** — what a person was afraid of, named plainly. The pillar that
   serves the frightened reader most directly.
2. **How I Was Treated** — the human conduct of care: being explained to, being
   remembered, being included. The campaign's centre.
3. **The Family's Account** — the experience of the relative who was there, standing
   on its own rather than as a supplement.
4. **What Was Hard** — the difficult parts, told honestly. Required in every story,
   and a territory in its own right.
5. **What I'd Tell Someone Else** — practical advice from someone who has been
   through it. The most useful content the campaign produces.

Note what is absent: there is no recovery pillar and no results pillar. That is
deliberate, and it is the structural expression of this campaign's central rule.

## Storytelling Opportunities

Moments, not topics:

- A patient describing the exact sentence someone said to them.
- A relative recalling the first update they were given.
- Someone naming a fear they had not told their family.
- A contributor laughing at how frightened they had been.
- A patient describing a wait honestly, and what made it bearable.
- The detail nobody would invent — a blanket, a window, a name.
- A family member recalling being walked somewhere rather than pointed.
- Someone saying they still think about a particular night.
- A contributor correcting an assumption they arrived with.
- The quiet gratitude that is not addressed to anyone in particular.

## Communication Style

Restrained. Plain. Unproduced.

Never: triumphant, sentimental, dramatic, or polished.

The contributor's voice, not the brand's. The brand should be almost absent from the
telling — its presence is the reason the story exists, not a participant in it.

## Tone of Voice

Write as someone who listened carefully and is passing on what they heard, without
improving it.

Resist every instinct to strengthen the ending. A story that trails off honestly is
more credible than one that lands.

## Messaging Rules

**Always**

- Obtain documented, specific, withdrawable consent before anything is produced.
- Obtain it outside the treating relationship and after care has concluded.
- Use the contributor's own words.
- Include at least one unflattering or difficult element.
- Have the contributor approve the final version.
- Record the consent reference against the published asset.
- Keep the focus on treatment, not outcome.
- Maintain a working withdrawal route.

**Never**

- Publish without documented consent and contributor approval.
- Compensate a contributor.
- Present an outcome as typical, expected or achievable.
- Use before-and-after clinical framing.
- Edit a quote to change its meaning or strengthen its sentiment.
- Publish a story from a patient in active care.
- Name another patient, or any staff member without their own consent.
- Use a story to promote a specific service, physician or offer.
- Publish a child's story without guardian consent plus additional review.
- Use a decedent's story without documented family consent and elevated review.
- Imply that care elsewhere would have been worse.
- Combine a story with any offer, discount or booking incentive.

# ═══════════ OFFERS ═══════════

## Current Offers

**No offers.**

No promotion, package or discount may appear in or beside a patient story. Attaching
an offer to a patient's account converts their generosity into a sales instrument
and destroys the credibility that is the campaign's only asset.

## Offer Rules

- No story is ever published alongside a promotion.
- No contributor is compensated, in money or in care.
- No story is used in paid advertising without consent that explicitly covers paid
  placement — organic consent does not extend to it.
- No story is used to promote the specific service the contributor received.

## Call To Action

In priority order:

1. **Learn More** — the default, and usually the only appropriate choice.
2. **Save Post**.
3. **Contact Us** — where a reader in a similar situation may want to ask something.

**Never `Book Now` on a patient story.** It converts a person's account into a sales
funnel and confirms every suspicion the audience already holds.

## Primary KPI

**Shares and saves.**

A story succeeds when it is sent to someone facing the same thing. That is the
campaign's purpose and its honest measure. Reach is a weak proxy, and engagement
rewards exactly the emotional intensity the campaign is trying to avoid.

# ═══════════ STRATEGIC GOVERNANCE ═══════════

## Strategic Reminder

Before publishing, two questions:

1. **Would this contributor be comfortable with this appearing in front of their
   employer, their neighbours and their family in five years?**
2. **Could a reader finish this expecting the same medical outcome?**

If the first is uncertain or the second is yes, it does not publish.

## Strategic Constraints

- Never publish without documented consent and contributor approval.
- Never compensate contributors.
- Never imply typicality or predict outcomes.
- Never use a patient's vulnerability as persuasive force.
- Never publish a story that would work but should not run.
- Professional ethics and patient dignity outrank marketing effectiveness, always.
- INSAN's long-term reputation outranks any campaign result.

## Medical Communication Principles

Every statement supportable. No clinical advice. No outcome prediction. Clinical
uncertainty respected. Where a contributor describes their own medical experience,
it is presented as their experience and never as a general claim.

Clinical review is mandatory on every story, specifically to catch outcome
implications the writer did not intend.

## Never Promise

- Any clinical outcome, explicitly or by implication.
- That a reader's experience will resemble the contributor's.
- That results shown are typical, expected or representative.
- Recovery, improvement, survival or a timeframe.
- That a specific physician or team will be involved in a reader's care.
- Availability of any service described in a story.
- That the ecosystem produces the outcome the story contains.
- Anything not documented in the relevant entity's own knowledge file.

## Can Promise

- That every story is true and told by the person who lived it.
- That consent was documented, specific and freely given.
- That contributors approved the final version and may withdraw.
- That no contributor was compensated.
- That difficult parts are included rather than removed.
- That the standard of care described is INSAN's, at both hospitals.

## Brand Perception

After sustained exposure, the audience should believe:

> *"Those are real people, and they're not all perfect stories. That's probably what
> it's actually like there."*

Perceived as: honest, restrained, dignified, credible, human.

Never as: manipulative, selective, salesy, exploitative, or sentimental.

## Long-Term Brand Contribution

This campaign is the evidence base for every experiential claim the ecosystem makes.
When a service campaign says families are kept informed, this is where the audience
has already heard a family say so.

Its value is entirely dependent on restraint. One story that reads as manufactured
retroactively devalues every story published before it, because the audience
re-reads the whole campaign as selection. The asset being built is credibility, and
credibility is spent by a single obvious exaggeration.

For Delta specifically, this campaign is the mechanism by which trust is rebuilt
through demonstrated experience rather than assertion — consistent with
`PROJECT_DECISIONS.md` §4, which requires trust messaging to be integrated into
ordinary content rather than published as explicit trust-rebuilding announcements.

## Relationship With INSAN

**Trust platform: Transparency.** Supported by Safety and Continuity.

Every story is evidence of the standard INSAN develops and both hospitals deliver.
The consistent element across stories is never the outcome — it is how people were
treated, which is what a platform standard actually governs.

Per `AI_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION.md` §16, cross-platform reinforcement stays contextual
and never interrupts the story. A patient's account is not the place to explain the
operating model.

# ═══════════ INSTITUTIONAL KNOWLEDGE ═══════════

## Institutional Knowledge Extensions

**Why this file is mostly prohibitions.** The default execution of a testimonial
campaign — best outcomes, grateful patients, emotional music — is effective in the
short term and corrosive over time, and it is what this campaign will drift toward
under any pressure to perform. The prohibitions are the campaign; the permissions
are the small remainder.

**Gratitude is not free consent.** The most important operational insight here. A
patient who feels indebted will agree to more than they are comfortable with and
will not say so. Every procedural rule — approach outside the care relationship,
delay after care concludes, contributor approval, withdrawal route — exists to
compensate for a power asymmetry that a consent form does not remove.

**Supply is the constraint, and it is protected by conduct.** Contributors come from
people who had a good experience and heard that the last contributor was treated
well. Mishandling one story does not cost one story; it costs the pipeline.

**The unflattering detail is a feature.** Marketing review will try to remove it
every time. It is the single element that makes the story credible to the sceptical
reader the campaign is written for, and removing it removes the campaign's reason to
exist.

**Withdrawal must actually work.** A consent regime with no functioning removal
route is not consent. The route must be documented, staffed and honoured without
argument, including for material already in paid distribution.

# ═══════════ AI & DOCUMENTATION ═══════════

## Knowledge Scope

Permanent knowledge about this campaign only. Not execution, scheduling or
performance reporting. Not the consent forms themselves, and not the individual
stories — those are assets, held with their consent records.

Where this file and a downstream document disagree, this file wins. Where this file
and `MASTER_BRAND_ARCHITECTURE.md` disagree, the architecture wins.

## AI Worker Guidance

Mistakes specific to this campaign, in likelihood order:

1. **Making the outcome the point.** The default failure and the most damaging.
   The story is about treatment.
2. **Removing the difficult part.** It reads as an improvement and it destroys the
   credibility that is the whole asset.
3. **Improving the quote.** Strengthening a sentiment changes a person's words into
   the brand's.
4. **Implying typicality.** "Like many of our patients" is prohibited.
5. **A booking CTA.** Converts a person's account into a funnel.
6. **Inventing a story.** A composite, illustrative or representative patient story
   is fabrication. If no consented story exists, the post does not exist.
